Centralization and tablet form

A plan that works through ShG's nine points, an SI table of twelve centralization parameters scored for seventeen period rows, and five pilot analyses run from reproducible code. For reading and correcting in the morning.

The article asks whether the state made the document, but it never says what "the state" was in each period or how strong it was, so its answer cannot be tested. This plan makes centralization a measured variable. Twelve parameters, drawn from established state-capacity and household-economy frameworks and scored from the current literature, place each period on a scale. The pilots then ask whether these scores predict how uniform, how tall and how shaped the tablets are.

    The nine points, one by one

    Each point gets how I understood it, what the rewrite does with it, and where it stands after tonight.

    POINT 1

    A theoretical framework and a comparative setting framework drafted

    The article now has diplomatics and distant viewing as its frame, which are methods, not a theory of why documents should be uniform. The rewrite sets out four competing explanations of uniformity, each from an established literature, and makes the corpus decide between them (see "The new central line").

    • The state (Weber's bureaucracy; Scott's legibility; Mann's infrastructural power): the more a polity extracts, counts and communicates in writing, the more uniform its documents.
    • The institution that trains and checks scribes (Schloen's patrimonial household; Gelb, Diakonoff and Renger on palace, temple and house sectors; Garfinkle on Ur III): uniformity follows the household or office, whatever the size of the polity.
    • The organisation of production (the "standardization hypothesis" of craft studies: Costin, Rice, Blackman, Stein and Vandiver at Tell Leilan, Roux): uniformity follows the intensity of production and the number of producers, not political control. Tablets are craft products, so this literature applies directly, and it already contains the warning that standardization is a weak index of centralization unless time and place are tightly controlled.
    • The function of the document (Radner; Veenhof; Steinkeller on archival practice): form follows document type, so a period looks uniform when it writes few types.

    The comparative setting: Seshat's coding of world polities (Turchin et al. 2018 in PNAS, the natural point of comparison for a PNAS readership), the political-science measures of state capacity and information capacity (Hanson and Sigman 2021; Brambor et al. 2020), and two documentary parallels to cite briefly: quantitative codicology of medieval manuscripts (Bozzolo and Ornato 1980) and the early Islamic Qur'an page (George 2010, already cited). A short paragraph on Qin and Han administrative documents would add a case where the state is known to have regulated document format, but I could not verify the slip-length regulations tonight and have left it out.

    POINT 2

    Why it is historically significant argument drafted

    The significance is that tablet form gives an independent, material and corpus-wide measure of how far an institution reached into the practice of its scribes. That bears on three live debates:

    • Whether Mesopotamian states were bureaucracies (Steinkeller 1987), patrimonial households writ large (Schloen 2001; Garfinkle 2008, 2022) or "low-power" states that claimed more than they could do (Richardson 2012, 2017). These positions rest on texts that the state itself wrote. Form is a trace the state did not compose.
    • Whether schooling or administration made the scribe: what school texts say against what scribes did (already in the Introduction).
    • Beyond Assyriology: Scott's claim that early states made the world legible through standardized records has never been tested on the longest written record of early states. A negative or conditional answer is as publishable as a positive one, if the measurement is sound.
    POINT 3

    A centralization scale for each regime, and a statistic of how form correlates with it scale scored · pilot run

    Twelve parameters in three dimensions (polity reach; institutional economy; control of writing and norms), each on a 0 to 3 scale with written anchors, are scored in the SI table below. They combine into three dimension scores and one overall index (first principal component, 46% of the variance). The pilot correlates each score with three properties of each period's archival tablets: uniformity of proportion, uniformity of the whole VAE silhouette, and the share of tall tablets, each also measured within museum.

    POINT 4

    At least ten parameters of centralization, in an SI table 12 parameters × 17 rows

    Strong temple, palatial sector, scribal school and house sector are all parameters (TEMP, PAL, SCHOOL, PRIV), joined by territorial scope, administrative tiers, written taxation, communication infrastructure, crown control of temples, standardized procedure, codified law and imposed standards. Each cell has a score, a confidence level, one sentence of evidence and references. 204 cells: 83 high, 92 medium and 29 low confidence.

    POINT 5

    Ratio per genre group: logistic regression on all parameters, by genre, archive and place running

    Two levels. Cells of period × findspot × genre group (administrative, legal, letters; 97 cells with at least 25 tablets) carry the uniformity outcomes. Tablets carry the binary outcome (tall or not) in a logistic regression with genre group and damage as controls. Because the parameters change only between periods, significance is judged by permuting the parameter rows across periods, which is the honest test with 15 periods.

    POINT 6

    Predict the whole VAE vector, not only the ratio running

    Read one way: the outcome becomes the twelve VAE dimensions (multivariate regression; the share of VAE variance a parameter explains beyond genre). Read the other way: train on all periods but one, predict the held-out period's centralization score from its tablets' VAE vectors, and ask whether the predictions rank the periods correctly.

    POINT 7

    Which parameter goes with which element of form pilot run

    Elements are proportion and four features recovered from the VAE (thickness at the sides and ends, rectangularity, corner fill; cross-validated R² 0.78, 0.72, 0.40, 0.41), each as a period median and a period spread. Of 170 parameter-element pairs, two reach p < 0.05 (palace weight with rounder, less rectangular outlines), about what chance would give. At the period level, no parameter has a clear signature in form.

    POINT 8

    Regime type and changes in tablet production, reproducibly pilot run

    Production is counted from the whole CDLI catalogue (411,729 records, dated tablets per year or reign), and format from the photographs. Three scales: Ur III year by year and province by province; Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid reign by reign; and periods (catalogued tablets per century). Regime milestones are marked: Puzriš-Dagan founded (Šulgi 39), the last two Ur III reigns, the Eanna takeover (about 584 BCE), Nabonidus, Cyrus (539), the revolts against Xerxes (484).

    POINT 9

    Legal texts before and after Hammurabi pilot run

    A difference-in-differences design: legal documents from the former kingdom of Larsa (Larsa, Ur, Nippur, Isin) against those of the kingdom of Babylon (Sippar, Kish, Dilbat, Babylon), before and after the conquest of 1763 BCE, dated by Archibab or by CDLI ruler and year (527 tablets). If the conquest brought a Babylonian norm, the south should move towards the north and the gap between them should close.

    The new central line

    Proposed question: What made cuneiform documents uniform: the reach of the state, the institution that trained and checked the scribe, the scale of production, or the type of document? The four answers make different predictions that the corpus can test.

    ExplanationFrameworkPredictionTest in our dataPilot verdict

    The likely shape of the paper's answer, if the full analysis confirms the pilots: uniformity does not rise with the reach of the polity. It appears where a single institution made most of the tablets (Šuruppak's great household, Girsu's Bau household, Puzriš-Dagan, Persepolis), and it changes when that institution changes (the Ur III provinces in the last two reigns, Eanna under Nabonidus). That is a precise and testable version of the current conclusion, "the state made the document where it also made the scribe", and it can be stated against named alternatives.

    The identification problem, and how the rewrite solves it

    Centralization scores change only between periods, so any period-level test has as many observations as periods. Three remedies, in order of strength:

    1. Within-period natural experiments, where the regime changed and the scribal milieu can be held constant. This is where the three case studies move: they stop being illustrations and become the main tests, with the parameters scored before and after each event (table below).
    2. Place-level scores where one period contains different regimes (Persepolis and Babylonia under the Achaemenids; Puzriš-Dagan against the provinces in Ur III; capital and provinces in Assyria).
    3. Period-level tests with permutation inference, reported as the comparative frame, with their low power stated.

    Comparison with Seshat (Equinox-2020)

    Seshat codes 15 of our rows through matching polities. Its territory agrees well with our territorial scores (Spearman 0.87, 13 rows), but its institutional variables cannot separate ancient Near Eastern periods: full-time bureaucrats, couriers and calendar are coded "present" for every matching polity, and its administrative levels put the Ur III state (4) below the Old Babylonian (6) and Neo-Assyrian below both (3.5). This is why the article needs its own codebook, built on Seshat's protocol (anchored scales, confidence, evidence per cell) but graded for the distinctions that matter within ancient Western Asia. The codebook could be offered back to Seshat as a contribution.

    Corrections found tonight

    • Widell 2004 does not support "the state calendar" (Sections 4 and 5, Discussion). Replace with taxation (Sharlach 2004) and standard bookkeeping (Molina 2016) as the evidence of integration, or with royal measures (the gur lugal).
    • Seshat codes the Neo-Assyrian empire without a formal legal code, which matches our LAW score but is worth a sentence, because readers outside Assyriology will expect empires to have codified law.
    • Weeden (OHANE III, 568-569) reads the Hittite instruction texts as reactions to instability whose effect is doubtful. If the Hittite tablets stay in the paper, their standardization should not be cited as evidence of strong control without that caveat.
