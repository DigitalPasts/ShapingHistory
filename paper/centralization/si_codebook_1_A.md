## 2. Dimension A: polity reach

Dimension A measures how far royal power extended and through which channels it acted. Its four parameters follow the sequence by which a ruler governs territory beyond the capital: the territory itself, the officials who govern it, the revenues taken from it, and the messengers who connect it.

### TERR: Territorial scope of the polity

**Definition.** The extent of the territory ruled by one king at the period's height: one city, several cities, a whole region, or several regions held as provinces or vassals.

**Why it matters.** Territorial reach is the classic measure of extensive power (Mann) and of social scale in Seshat. If centralization produces uniform tablets, wider rule should spread one format over more scribes.

**Origin.** Seshat *Polity territory*; Mann 1986 (extensive power); the debate on whether the Sargonic and Ur III states were empires or hegemonies of city-states.

| Score | Anchor | Operational test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | single city-state | Only one city and its hinterland under the ruler; neighbouring cities independent. |
| 1 | multi-city regional state or league | Several cities under one ruler or bound in a league, within one region. |
| 2 | territorial kingdom unifying a region | One king rules a whole region, for example all of Sumer and Akkad or all of Assyria. |
| 3 | multi-regional empire with provinces or vassals | Several regions held as provinces or by vassal treaty, beyond the home region. |

**Records that show it.** Seshat polity territory (km2); number of cities under one ruler.

**Decision rules and boundary cases.**

- Score the polity whose tablets dominate the corpus for that period, at its documented height, not its founding.
- A commercial network is not territory. Old Assyrian trade colonies in Anatolia did not belong to the city of Assur, so Old Assyrian scores 0.
- A league of cities (ED IIIa Šuruppak) scores 1, not 2: the cities keep their own rulers.
- Early Old Babylonian is a period of competing kingdoms (Isin, Larsa, Ešnunna, Babylon); score the typical kingdom, which is 1; Ešnunna's wider influence (De Graef 2022 p. 424) is the upper bound.

**Across periods.** Ur III (3) and Early Old Babylonian (1) show the scale's span within a few generations: the same cities passed from provinces of one empire to the capitals of rival kingdoms. Hammurabi's conquests return Old Babylonian to 3.

### HIER: Administrative tiers under royal appointees

**Definition.** The number of administrative tiers between the king and the local community that were staffed by royal appointees.

**Why it matters.** Tiers of appointed officials are the channel through which a central format could be imposed on provincial scribes.

**Origin.** Seshat *Administrative levels*; Weber's bureaucratic hierarchy; debate on the Ur III ensi2 as royal governors versus local dynasts.

| Score | Anchor | Operational test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | one tier (household or city) | A single tier: the city or the great household runs itself. |
| 1 | capital + dependent towns | The capital administers dependent towns directly; no appointed governors. |
| 2 | appointed provincial governors | Provincial governors or equivalents appointed by the king. |
| 3 | four or more tiers (provinces/satrapies, districts, local) with appointees | Four or more tiers (province, district, town, institution) with royal appointees at each. |

**Records that show it.** Seshat administrative levels; attested titles of provincial officials.

**Decision rules and boundary cases.**

- Count only offices filled by royal appointment, not hereditary local rulers who acknowledge a king.
- Where governors are appointed but come from local families (Ur III ensi2), score 2 or 3 depending on the number of tiers documented, and record the qualification.
- Neo-Assyrian is contested (2-3): Baker 2023 points to the era of powerful magnates before Tiglath-pileser III. Neo-Babylonian is contested (2-3): Jursa 2023 p. 113 finds local power more decentralized than in Assyria, although governors and chief priests were royal appointees (p. 118).

**Across periods.** Old Akkadian is contested (2-3): Sargon appointed governors (Schrakamp 2020; Yoffee 1995 p. 292), but the lower ranks stayed local and imperial control was loose (Charpin 1995 p. 810). Ur III scores 3.

### FISC: Fiscal extraction recorded in writing

**Definition.** Regular extraction of revenue for the crown that is recorded in writing: from ad hoc tribute to an integrated, centrally recorded system across provinces.

**Why it matters.** Fiscal extraction is the core of state capacity (Monson and Scheidel 2015). Recorded levies need standardized documents, which could favour uniform tablet formats.

**Origin.** No Seshat equivalent; Monson and Scheidel 2015; Hanson and Sigman 2021; Assyriological work on bala, gun2 ma-da, ilkum and satrapal taxation.

| Score | Anchor | Operational test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | none beyond institutional income | No revenue beyond the income of the institutions themselves. |
| 1 | ad hoc tribute and gifts | Ad hoc tribute, booty and gifts. |
| 2 | regular levies on land or service, recorded locally | Regular levies on land, produce or service, recorded locally. |
| 3 | integrated, centrally recorded system across provinces | An integrated system, recorded centrally, across provinces (for example Ur III bala and gun2 ma-da). |

**Records that show it.** named tax or levy systems (bala, gun2 ma-da, ilkum, ginā'u, sāḫu, satrapal taxes) and their archives.

**Decision rules and boundary cases.**

- The levy must be recorded in writing; levies known only from narrative sources score at most 2.
- Temple offerings count only when the crown imposes and records them, not when temples receive gifts.
- Hellenistic royal taxation recorded on parchment still counts; Messina 2009 p. 179 notes that taxed contracts moved from clay to sealed parchment under Antiochus I.

**Across periods.** ED IIIa scores 2 on a single statement about corvée levied by labour managers (Bartash 2020); ED IIIb on taxes levied on temple land at Lagaš (Yoffee 1995 p. 289) and silver used for tax payments (Bartash 2020). Ur III, Old Babylonian, Middle Assyrian and the first-millennium empires reach 3.

### COMM: Royal communication infrastructure

**Definition.** The infrastructure by which the crown moved messages and agents: occasional messengers, a regular royal messenger service, or relay stations and roads documented by ration records.

**Why it matters.** Infrastructural power (Mann 1984) depends on communication; a fast royal network carries both orders and scribal norms.

**Origin.** Seshat *Couriers* and *Postal stations*; Mann 1984; Sallaberger on Ur III messenger texts; Radner on the Neo-Assyrian royal road.

| Score | Anchor | Operational test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | none known | No royal communication known. |
| 1 | ad hoc messengers | Ad hoc messengers, sent case by case. |
| 2 | regular royal messenger service | A regular royal messenger service. |
| 3 | relay stations or road network documented by ration records | Relay stations or a road network, documented by ration or travel records. |

**Records that show it.** messenger texts, road stations, travel rations.

**Decision rules and boundary cases.**

- Score the crown's network, not merchants' private correspondence (Old Assyrian letters score 1).
- Letters alone show messengers (1); a service needs repeated, administered dispatch (2); stations need ration records at fixed points (3).

**Across periods.** ED IIIb scores 1 on one weak statement (Frahm 2015 p. 5: the earliest letters date to the 24th century); it is the thinnest cell in the main analysis. Ur III, Neo-Assyrian and Achaemenid score 3.
