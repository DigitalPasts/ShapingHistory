**Significance.** For three thousand years the clay tablet was the main written record of ancient Western Asia, yet its form has been described one tablet at a time. Measuring the outline of nearly 88,000 photographed tablets, we show that the form of a document followed the institution that trained and checked its scribes more than the state that ruled them. The provinces of the Ur III state converged only late, Neo-Assyrian scribes kept varied formats under a strong empire, and letters sent to the Pharaoh followed the place where the clay was formed. The approach makes diplomatics, the study of how documents were made, a measurable historical science, and the released measures give proportion, thickness and corner shape for tablets that no catalogue describes.

**Abstract.** Did the states of ancient Western Asia shape how their scribes made documents? Historians have long said so, on the evidence of a few hundred tablets at a time. We test the claim on 87,783 photographed cuneiform tablets in the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative, from the late fourth millennium to the first century BCE, each reduced to a silhouette without signs or surface. Silhouettes identify a tablet's period in 57% of cases across twelve periods, but part of this signal is the photography of the museum that holds it, a modern layer of the archive that we control throughout. Measured on 60,207 documents, the tall tablet was a Babylonian norm from the Sargonic to the Old Babylonian period, while wide tablets appear in Elam around 1400 BCE and in Babylonia only in the first millennium. Uniformity does not simply follow the state. The most uniform tablets come from Early Dynastic Šuruppak and Adab, before any territorial state; the Ur III provinces converged only in the last two reigns; and Neo-Assyrian documents, made under the strongest state of their time, are the most varied. Three case studies place the norm at different levels: the state in Ur III, the place where the tablet was made in the Amarna letters, and the temple office at Eanna under Nabonidus. Tablet form records what scribes did rather than what they said about their craft.

**Keywords:** cuneiform | diplomatics | distant viewing | scribal practice | variational autoencoder

# Introduction

Did the states of ancient Western Asia shape how their scribes made documents? Historians of nearly every period of cuneiform writing have said yes, usually in passing. The Ur III state (2110 to 2003 BCE) is described as a bureaucracy whose uniform paperwork reflects central control [@steinkellerAdministrativeEconomicOrganization1987], while others read the same records as the work of households whose practices the state merely scaled up [@garfinkleWasUrIII2008]. In the second millennium BCE, Middle Assyrian administrative tablets are read as the instruments of a state bureaucracy with shared scribal norms [@postgateMiddleAssyrianTablets1986; @cancik-kirschbaumMiddleAssyrian2012], and Hittite tablet formats as conventions of the royal chancery at Ḫattuša [@waalHittiteDiplomaticsStudies2015]. Neo-Assyrian letters are said to keep one format across archives [@radnerRelationFormat1995], and Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid archival documents have been read as evidence of institutional norms and of their change under empire [@hacklMaterialienZurUrkundenlehre2013; @jursaDiplomaticsLateBabylonian2021]. Each claim rests on expert inspection of a few hundred tablets from any one period. None has been tested across the history of the script, so none can tell whether uniformity follows the state or comes before it.

The difficulty lies partly in the sources. What we know about scribal training comes mostly from what scribes and rulers chose to write about it: school compositions, lexical lists, colophons and royal hymns [@wilckeWerUndSchrieb2000; @charpinReadingWriting2010; @veldhuisLevelsLiteracy2011]. These texts tell us what scribes claimed about their education, not how they worked. The tablets themselves are evidence of another kind. Each was shaped from clay by a trained hand before any writing started, so its form is a trace of practice rather than a statement about it. Read one at a time, such traces remain detached from their *Sitz im Leben*. Read together, they become a record of how writing was taught and governed.

Reading them together is what distant viewing makes possible. Arnold and Tilton define it as the systematic, explicit and reproducible application of computational methods to large visual corpora, so that patterns invisible in the close viewing of any single object become measurable [@arnoldTiltonDistantViewing2019; @arnoldTiltonDistantViewingBook2023]. Unlike distant reading of texts [@morettiDistantReading2013], it requires the researcher to build a code system before the analysis begins, deciding what to measure and what to discard. We start from the corpus seen whole and only then turn to the archive and the single tablet: the telescope before the microscope [@gordinMattersGenreStyle2025]. Our code system is deliberately minimal. Each tablet is reduced to its silhouette, without signs, text or surface. If silhouettes alone carry historical signal, then shape itself is historical evidence.

There are good reasons to expect that they do. Preparing and shaping the clay was part of the scribal craft and of scribal education. In the Old Babylonian schools of Nippur and elsewhere, tablet format changed with the stage of training and with the genre being copied [@veldhuisCuneiformTabletEducational1996; @veldhuisLevelsLiteracy2011; @delneroArchivesLibrariesOld2019], and particular tablet types were tied to particular uses throughout the history of cuneiform [@taylorTabletsArtefactsScribes2011]. Format is therefore not incidental to the text. It records a learned norm, and the degree to which a group of scribes kept to that norm measures the reach of their training.

Reading institutions from the external form of documents is the founding idea of diplomatics. Mabillon's *De re diplomatica* (1681) set out criteria for judging charters by their script, formulae, material and seals [@mabillonDeReDiplomatica1681], and the discipline later grew into a general science of how records are made and of what their form reveals about the authority behind them [@durantiDiplomaticsNew1998]. Assyriologists have brought this lens to cuneiform archives, describing format by genre, archive and period [@radnerRelationFormat1995; @hacklMaterialienZurUrkundenlehre2013; @jursaDiplomaticsLateBabylonian2021]. The approach has two limits. Its descriptive terms, such as "cushion-shaped" or "convex-plano", resist systematic comparison, and its corpora rarely exceed what one expert can examine. Distant viewing addresses both, turning the expert's categories into explicit measures and applying them to every photographed tablet.

We apply this approach to 87,783 photographed tablets in the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative, dating from the late fourth millennium to the first century BCE [@cdliContributors2024]. We ask three questions of their silhouettes. Can a tablet's period be read from its silhouette alone? How did tablet proportions change over three millennia? And how uniform was each period, site and archive, measured both by the ratio of height to width and in a twelve-dimensional shape space learned by a variational autoencoder [VAE; @kingmaVAE2013]? We test the answers in three case studies that each hold the type of document constant while something else changes: the provinces of the Ur III state (2110 to 2003 BCE), the letters sent to the Pharaoh from across Western Asia in the fourteenth century BCE, and the archive of the Eanna temple at Uruk under the Neo-Babylonian kings (626 to 539 BCE). Silhouettes discard the surface cues that inflate the accuracy of earlier image classifiers [@chenCuneiML2023; @chenDistributionShift2024], but not the habits of the museums that photographed the tablets, and we control for these throughout.

Four results emerge. Silhouettes identify the period of a tablet, and Ur III tablets are recognised whichever museum holds them. Tall tablets were the Babylonian norm from the Sargonic to the Old Babylonian period, and Babylonian documents became wide only in the first millennium BCE. Uniformity does not simply follow the state: the most uniform tablets were made before any territorial state, the Ur III provinces converged only in the last two reigns, and Neo-Assyrian documents are the most varied of all. And the case studies place the norm at three levels, the state, the place where the tablet was made, and the scribal office.

We begin where distant viewing begins, with the corpus seen whole (Fig. 1): which tablets survive, how each photograph becomes a silhouette, and how the corpus falls across periods, sites and genres. We then take the three measures in turn, and close with the case studies and what they say about state and scribe.

# Results

## The corpus through the telescope

Seen whole, the photographed record of cuneiform consists of 87,783 tablets in the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative, of which 87,221 fall in seventeen periods with at least 100 tablets each, from Uruk III (c. 3200 BCE) to the Hellenistic period [@cdliContributors2024; Fig. 1]. The numbers are very uneven. Four periods, Ur III, Neo-Assyrian, Old Babylonian and Middle Babylonian, account for 82% of the tablets, while six periods have fewer than 500 each. Genres are just as uneven. CDLI assigns them along the conventional Assyriological division between archival documents (administrative, legal, letters) and the literary and scholarly texts of the "stream of tradition" [@oppenheimAncientMesopotamia1977; @pedersenArchivesLibraries1998]. Administrative documents make up 48% of all tablets and more than 85% of every period before 2000 BCE, while a quarter of the tablets, among them 65% of the Neo-Assyrian ones, have no genre assigned at all. An average over the whole corpus is therefore mostly an average of administrative tablets from four periods, and we compare like with like: uniformity is measured within administrative tablets wherever their numbers allow.

The shape of the corpus is also the shape of what survived and of how it was found. Cuneiform tablets are unevenly spread in time and space, because of ancient conditions of preservation and destruction as much as chance discoveries, excavation priorities and the antiquities market, which filled museums with tablets from a few sites [@rattenborgArchaeologicalDistributionCuneiform2023; @michelIdentifyingMissingEvidence2025]. Old Assyrian is the clearest case. 99% of its tablets come from Kanesh in Anatolia, where the merchants' archives were preserved in their houses in the lower town, while the contemporary archives of Assur itself remain largely unexcavated [@larsenAncientKanesh2015]. In the same way, 96% of Neo-Assyrian tablets come from Nineveh, 83% of Hittite tablets from Ḫattuša and 77% of Middle Assyrian tablets from Assur. Another 15% of all tablets have no recorded findspot. For many of these, the findspot can be reconstructed from publications, museum records or internal evidence such as month names and prosopography [@brumfieldKeyCity2019], but this is an extra step between the catalogue and any historical inference of the kind attempted here. Where one site dominates, a period's profile may be the profile of one institution. Middle Babylonian tablets, for example, come 78% from Nippur, so what we measure as Middle Babylonian practice is largely the practice of Nippur's administration, which might not represent the norm of the Kassite state. We therefore compare periods where several sites contribute, and sites within a period wherever we can.

The tablets also reach us in very different states. Most cuneiform tablets are chipped, cracked or abraded, and many are fragments. Rather than set broken tablets aside, which would leave almost no Neo-Assyrian material, we treat preservation as a variable, much as epigraphers read a damaged text against its parallels. A damage index computed from each silhouette's outline separates tablets catalogued as complete from fragments (AUC 0.92). Damage is unevenly spread: 4% of Ur III tablets fall in the most damaged third of the corpus, against 68% of Neo-Assyrian and 85% of Hittite tablets. It lowers the accuracy of period identification from 0.85 for the least damaged tablets to 0.74 for the most damaged, but controlling for it leaves the ranking of periods by uniformity unchanged.

Our code system reduces each photograph to a black-and-white silhouette of all its views, usually obverse, reverse and four edges. Signs, surface, colour and background are discarded. What remains is the outline the scribe shaped, together with whatever the photographer's framing added to it, a point we return to in the next section [@chenDistributionShift2024]. Photographs give proportions, not sizes. CDLI does record sizes in millimetres for 17,568 of our tablets, however, and for these the photographic and physical height-to-width ratios agree closely (Spearman 0.77), though less so for broken tablets. All catalogue data were checked against the live CDLI database in October 2026. Since the period labels used here were fixed in April 2024, CDLI has re-dated 0.85% of the tablets, which leaves every period's measured uniformity practically unchanged. The extraction steps, their known errors and the full period table are in Materials and Methods and SI Table S1.

## What silhouettes tell us about chronology

Assyriologists have long dated tablets by their look before reading them, and the physical form of a tablet is known to correlate with its date and function [@taylorTabletsArtefactsScribes2011; @radnerRelationFormat1995]. The proportion of height to width matters for cuneiform diplomatics because it is a choice the scribe made before writing: it fixes how many lines fit on a face, whether the text runs across the long or the short side, and how the tablet sits in the hand. Its distribution over three millennia is the subject of the next section. What has been missing, as Taylor notes, is a comprehensive account of tablet form across the whole record. A classifier trained on the silhouettes offers one. Asked to name the period of a tablet it has not seen, it is right 57% of the time across twelve historical periods, from Early Dynastic IIIb (c. 2500 BCE) to the Achaemenid empire (331 BCE; Fig. 2).

**Museums before periods.** The silhouettes can also reveal which museum holds a tablet: among Ur III tablets from Umma, for example, the model names the right museum three times out of four (chance is one in five). Two histories lie behind this insight: CDLI's images were made over two decades using different standards, including scanning, studio photography and museums' own photographs [@dahlLookingForwardBack2019; @chenClassificationPaleographic2024], and excavation, the antiquities market and dispersed archives sent the tablets of one period and city to a few museums [@charpinPublicationDocumentsArchives2026; @deboerDesertDesk2026]. Tested only on museums it never saw, the classifier falls from 57% to 30%. Ur III tablets are still recognised (72% before, 69% after), as are, in part, Old Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian and Old Babylonian ones. Neo-Assyrian recognition collapses from 86% to almost nothing: 99% of these tablets are in the British Museum, and the model had learned that museum's images, labelling 30% of its other tablets Neo-Assyrian. Ur III is the clearest case of a shape norm shared across collections; for Neo-Assyrian we cannot yet tell shape from photography. The ranking of periods by uniformity survives the museum control (SI Section S3).

**Calibrating the chronological signal.** The Achaemenid tablets from Persepolis are recognised 94% of the time, those from Babylonia only 33%, since most of the latter look Neo-Babylonian, in line with the continuity of Babylonian scribal practice under Persian rule [@waerzeggers_marduk-remanni_2014; @jursaDiplomaticsLateBabylonian2021]. In Early Old Babylonian, only royal inscriptions and cones are recognised, but not everyday documents, so the label marks a dynastic division [@charpinMesopotamienAltbabylonische2004] rather than a change in how ordinary tablets were made.

**Inside one period: shape follows the document.** Old Babylonian comprises several scribal traditions, with schools that differed from city to city [@robsonTabletHouseScribal2001; @tanretAsperaAdAstra2002; @wagensonnerLarsaSchoolsPalaeographic2019; @delneroArchivesLibrariesOld2019]. We linked 2,479 photographed Old Babylonian tablets to the Archibab database of Old Babylonian archives [@charpinAssyriologistComputerArchibab2014], queried in October 2026, and compared tablets that share a label but sit in different museums (Fig. 3). Shape follows document type most closely: loans, receipts and leases from different cities and museums are markedly more similar than chance, letters and purchases less so. Specialists recognise a loan or a sale at a glance, and even small private archives standardised their formats [@goddeerisShapeSizeArchival2023]. What is new is that this can be measured across cities and collections, and that it outweighs city and reign. Archive comes next. The tablets of Šamaš-hazir, who managed the royal domain of Larsa for Hammurabi after 1763 BCE [@fietteArchibab3Palais2018], are now in six museums, yet resemble each other more than other tablets of the same types in those museums. Across museums, only Larsa and the Hammurabi years stand out among cities and reigns; within one museum, tablets of the same type from the same city are more alike, so a local habit sits on top of the shared form. The counter-example is expenditure lists and summary accounts, which show no shared form. Text length explains at most a seventh of their variation in shape. More likely, these modern scholarly categories group records that scribes kept apart, such as single disbursements and monthly or yearly summaries, which at Nippur differ in form [@goddeerisShapeSizeArchival2023] (SI Section S4). A millennium later the pattern recurs: Neo-Babylonian promissory notes now in different museums also resemble each other more than chance would predict [@waerzeggersProsobabProsopographyBabylonia2019].

**Regional lines rather than sequential genealogies.** Within Babylonia, later letters and legal documents are as close to the Old Babylonian forms as any other tablets of their period, once museum is controlled. The one exception is small: Kassite letters from Nippur in the Penn Museum (e.g. BE 17/1, PBS 1/2) are slightly closer to the Old Babylonian letter shapes than other Kassite tablets from Nippur. Where tablets do resemble those of another period, the resemblance follows regions (Fig. 4). The clearest case confirms what historians expect. The administrative tablets of Middle Elamite Anšan (Tall-i Malyan) find almost a quarter of their closest matches in the Persepolis Fortification archive, nine times the expected share; Malyan 59, for example, is closest to PF 102 [@stolperTextsTalliMalyan1984]. Achaemenid administration in Fārs continued Elamite practice [@henkelmanAchaemenidHeartland2012], and its tablets carry that inheritance in their outline across six centuries. A northern line appears as well. Old Assyrian letters from Kanesh are matched with Middle Assyrian letters from Assur nine times as often as chance predicts, though such matches remain few (7 in 100; e.g. TCL 19, 65 is closest to the Assur letter VS 1, 105). The Middle Babylonian tablets of Nuzi resemble Middle Assyrian ones three times as often as expected [@postgateMiddleAssyrianTablets1986], and those of Ḫattuša are closer to their Babylonian and Assyrian contemporaries twice as often. Old Babylonian letters from Susa, such as MDP 18, 246, find most of their closest matches among later tablets, Kassite ones from Nippur and Neo-Babylonian ones from Borsippa and Uruk. The Amarna letters show no particular affinity, and the Alalakh tablets' resemblance to Ur III follows the British Museum rather than history. These groups are small, and regional traditions such as Emar's two tablet formats [@flemingTabletTerminologyEmar2009] have too few photographs to test. Genealogy, then, runs along regional lines rather than from period to period within Babylonia. Tests and further examples are in SI Section S4.

## Tablet proportions across three millennia

It is well known that cuneiform tablets grew wider over time: third-millennium administrative tablets are tall (portrait / Hochformat), first-millennium Babylonian documents are wide (landscape / Querformat) [@taylorTabletsArtefactsScribes2011; @radnerRelationFormat1995]. Measured on 60,207 administrative, legal and epistolary tablets, the picture is more articulated than a single shift from tall to wide (Fig. 5). Literary, lexical and school tablets follow other rules and are treated in SI Section S5.4. The tall tablet is a norm of the Sargonic to Old Babylonian centuries, with medians of 1.21 (Old Akkadian), 1.13 (Ur III) and 1.28 (Old Babylonian). Before it, the Early Dynastic tablets of Šuruppak, Girsu and Ebla were nearly square (1.00 to 1.02); we use "tall", "wide" and "nearly square" in these senses throughout. After it, Middle Babylonian tablets return to the square (0.95), and only in the first millennium do Babylonian documents become wide (Neo-Babylonian 0.73, Achaemenid 0.77). Other traditions kept their own lines. Assyrian tablets are square from the Old Assyrian letters of Kaneš (1.02) through the Middle Assyrian period (1.02). The Middle Elamite tablets of Haft Tepe (ancient Kabnak) are already wide around 1400 BCE (0.77), seven centuries before Babylonia adopts this format [@herreroHaftTepeChoix1990]. These are the regional lines that the silhouettes revealed above, from Kaneš to Middle Assyrian Aššur and from Anšan to Persepolis.

**How strict each norm was.** The uniformity of a period, measured as the typical distance of its tablets from the period median (SI Section S5.2), tells us how closely scribes followed its norm. The Early Dynastic tablets are the most uniform of all (ED IIIa 0.038, mostly from Šuruppak and Adab; ED IIIb 0.065, mostly from Girsu, Zabalam and Umma), followed by first-millennium Babylonia (Hellenistic 0.056, Achaemenid 0.067, Neo-Babylonian 0.080) and Ur III (0.079). The ranking holds within museum, within findspot and across levels of damage. Neo-Assyrian documents are the most varied (0.36). As Radner showed, Neo-Assyrian letters and legal documents are tall and administrative records wide [@radnerRelationFormat1995], and our measurements confirm it (1.34, 1.27 and 0.86). The Ur III case shows what lies behind a uniform period. Puzriš-Dagan, the crown's livestock agency founded under Šulgi [@tsouparopoulouReconstructionPuzrisDagan2013], made the smallest and most uniform tablets of the period; a typical receipt measures 25 × 27 mm (Nisaba 8, 299, British Museum). Girsu and Umma, by contrast, kept formats of their own for the same kind of text. A Girsu messenger text is tall and narrow (HLC 163, 45 × 30 mm, ISAC Chicago), its Umma counterpart square (Nisaba 27, 228, 28 × 26 mm, Yale Babylonian Collection) [@manderMessengerTextsGirsu2008; @dagostinoUmmaMessengerTexts2008]. The difference is not the same for every type: Girsu loans are wide (Nisaba 10, 91, 36 × 39 mm, British Museum) where Umma loans are slightly tall (BCT 2, 80, 39 × 37 mm). Over five reigns the two provinces converged. Compared within the same type of text, as labelled in the Database of Neo-Sumerian Texts or identified by the bookkeeping terms of each tablet [@molinaDatabaseNeoSumerianTexts; @sigristDrehem1992], the difference between them shrinks steadily from the first decades of Šulgi to Ibbi-Suen, when it disappears (SI Section S5.5). We can now say with confidence that format followed the integration of the provinces into the state, as is known for their calendars and taxes [@widellCalendarNeoSumerianUr2004; @sharlachProvincialTaxationUr2004; @molinaArchivesBookkeepingSouthern2016]. Neo-Babylonian uniformity has another source. Promissory notes, receipts and leases from different archives and cities share one wide format [@jursaDiplomaticsLateBabylonian2021; @waerzeggersProsobabProsopographyBabylonia2019], so one form served every kind of document. Old Babylonian is less uniform for the opposite reason: it had two formats. Records of a single transaction, such as letter-orders, receipts and loans, are near square, while letters and documents about land and persons, such as purchases, leases, lawsuits and inheritance divisions, are tall (a receipt in OECT 15, 189 at 1.07 against a purchase in OECT 15, 268 at 1.58, both from Larsa in the Ashmolean Museum). Model contracts used in the Nippur school taught these document types [@spadaSumerianModelContracts2018], which may explain why each type kept its form across cities.

**Format and storage.** Veenhof observed that archivists could sort tablets by their size, shape and layout, because these went with the subject matter, and that the link has to be analysed per period [@veenhofCuneiformArchivesIntroduction1986]. Our measurements provide that analysis and show that the link took different forms. In Ur III, single-transaction tablets were kept in labelled baskets [@nelsonPisanDubBaTexts1976] and summarised in balanced accounts [@molinaArchivesBookkeepingSouthern2016]; the small, uniform receipt and the large, tall account mirror these two levels of bookkeeping. In the Old Babylonian period, the tall family consists of documents kept as evidence, often sealed in envelopes and stored in jars at home [@veenhofCuneiformArchivesIntroduction1986; @vanlerbergheStorageOldBabylonian1993], whereas the square records of single transactions served for a shorter time. In first-millennium Babylonia, where one wide format served every type, shape no longer told the archivist what a document was.

**Scribes fixing the proportions.** Manuscript scholars speak of a fixed balance when a scribe keeps the proportion of a page whatever its size [@georgeGeometryEarlyQuranic2010]. To test this, we ranked the documents of each period by size and divided them into five equal groups (Fig. 6; SI Section S5.3). Cuneiform scribes kept a fixed balance in some periods and not in others. In Early Dynastic Girsu the small and the large tablet are the same square (VS 14, 22 at 35 × 35 mm and VS 14, 57 at 85 × 85 mm, Vorderasiatisches Museum), and in the first millennium the wide format also keeps its proportion at every size. From the Sargonic period to the Old Babylonian period, however, scribes held the width and let the height grow with the text. Old Babylonian documents vary in width only between about 31 and 55 mm but in height between 32 and 91 mm, so the largest fifth is 1.7 times taller than wide while the smallest fifth is square. In Ur III the proportion stays fixed for all but the largest fifth, the large accounts and lists, which are tall (1.47). It therefore seems likely that a tablet that could be held in one hand set the width, whereas the text set the height. This is a measurable difference in how scribes planned a document before writing, and it separates the Babylonian centuries of the tall tablet (about 2350 to 1600 BCE) from the periods before and after them.

Right: the median height-to-width ratio of each group.](../../figures/Fig6_fixed_balance.png){#fig-fixed-balance}

**Counter-examples.** Not every group follows its period. Ebla, in the Early Dynastic period, is square but much more varied than Šuruppak or Girsu (0.19). The Achaemenid tablets of Persepolis follow a format of their own. And Old Babylonian literary and school tablets, unlike the documents, keep their proportion as they grow (SI Section S5.4).

**From period norms to archives.** Proportion is only one dimension of format, yet it already shows that a period's norm is the sum of many local decisions. Scribal habit, administrative practice and political integration each left their mark, and they pulled in different directions in different periods: in Ur III the state drew the provincial formats together, in the Old Babylonian period the type of document mattered more than the city, and in first-millennium Babylonia one format served every purpose. Separating these strands requires the study of particular archives and genres. The next section does this for three case studies, using the whole silhouette rather than proportion alone.

## Three archives: state, court and temple

**The state and its provinces: Ur III.** The provincial bureaucracies of Girsu, Umma and Puzriš-Dagan kept their own conventions, and their messenger texts have been studied province by province [@sharlachProvincialTaxationUr2004; @manderMessengerTextsGirsu2008; @dagostinoUmmaMessengerTexts2008]. Girsu and Umma also kept their own proportions for the same type of text, and the silhouette shows this more sharply. Among the messenger texts of the Harvard Museum of the Ancient Near East, labelled as such in the Database of Neo-Sumerian Texts [@molinaDatabaseNeoSumerianTexts], the silhouette assigns a tablet to Girsu or Umma correctly in 93 cases out of 100, against 83 for the ratio alone and 50 by chance (137 tablets from each province). A Girsu messenger text is tall, narrow and, for its width, thick (MVN 11, 109, 44 × 32 mm, late Šulgi); its Umma counterpart is nearly square and thinner (Nisaba 27, 12, 31 × 29 mm, Šū-Suen). Receipts show what the ratio misses. At Harvard, receipts from Puzriš-Dagan and Girsu have the same proportion (1.10 and 1.11), yet the silhouette separates them in 83 cases out of 100: the Puzriš-Dagan receipts have square corners, the Girsu receipts rounded ones. The signal is not universal. In the New York Public Library, receipts from Umma and Puzriš-Dagan are separated only weakly, and at ISAC Chicago the height and width in millimetres recorded in the CDLI catalogue separate them better than the silhouette does (SI Section S6.1; the photographs carry no scale, so size in millimetres can only come from the catalogue). Within the same text type, the distance between the Girsu and Umma silhouettes stays the same from Šulgi to Amar-Suen and falls by a third under Šū-Suen and by two thirds under Ibbi-Suen. The convergence of proportions was thus a convergence of the whole tablet, and it came in the last two reigns, when the crown's hold on the provinces is documented through the bala taxation and the state calendar [@sharlachProvincialTaxationUr2004; @widellCalendarNeoSumerianUr2004].

**Writing at the edge of the Babylonian world: the Amarna letters.** Two results on the Amarna letters have been established. The Amarna letters edition of Moran fixes the sender of most letters [@moranAmarnaLetters1992]. The petrographic study of Goren, Finkelstein and Na'aman located the clay of 274 letters: the great kings wrote at home, most Canaanite rulers used local clay, some letters were written at the Egyptian centres of Gaza and Beth-Shean, and the kings of Amurru moved from the slopes of Mount Lebanon to Ardata, Irqata and Sumur [@gorenPetrographicInvestigationAmarna2002; @gorenInscribedClayProvenance2004]. Badreshany and Philip, reviewing petrography in Levantine archaeology, treat this study as a reference for the clays of the Late Bronze Age Levant, but also mark the limits of the method: some neighbouring fabrics cannot be told apart under the microscope (their example is the pottery of Arqa and of the Byblos region), and provenance work gains most when it is joined to the visible, macroscale features of how an object was made [@badreshanyCeramicStudiesPetrographic2020]. The form of a tablet is such a feature, and the observations below add it to the clay evidence. Philologists, for their part, group the letters by scribe, with one to four scribes writing for a city in a generation [@vitaCanaaniteScribesAmarna2015; @cohenCuneiformWriting2018]. We add three observations on the 198 photographed letters, 176 of them in the Vorderasiatisches Museum, Berlin, and map them together with the clay analysis (Fig. 7; coordinates from Pleiades [@pleiades]; all letters, with their clay group and its reliability, in SI Dataset S3).

First, a scribe writing to the Pharaoh used a taller tablet than for letters at home. Babylonian letters sent to Egypt (1.53, six letters) are taller than the Middle Babylonian letters found at Nippur (1.33, 239 letters), and Mittani letters (1.54, four letters) are much taller than the letters of Nuzi, a city of the Mittani realm (1.06, 16 letters). Both pairs point the same way, although the numbers are small. Aššur may follow the same pattern. EA 15, the letter of Aššur-uballiṭ I, is not in CDLI, but the Metropolitan Museum of Art publishes its measurements (7.7 × 5.5 cm, a ratio of 1.40) and an open-access photograph, on which we measure 1.37 (MMA 24.2.11) [@metEA15]. Middle Assyrian letters found at Aššur have a ratio of 1.10 (12 letters). The other Assyrian letter, EA 16, in the Egyptian Museum (Cairo) is not photographed and could not be measured.

Second, the letters made in one place resemble each other. Placed where Goren and his colleagues located their clay, 131 Berlin letters fall into 17 places with three or more letters, and these differ in proportion (SI Section S6.2): the letters made at Byblos are tall (1.49, 36 letters), and the two well-preserved letters made at Jerusalem taller still (EA 286, 1.87; EA 287, 2.18), while the letters made in the Bašan (1.14), the Beqaʿ (1.21) and at Gaza (1.19) are barely taller than wide. The silhouette groups the letters more closely than the ratio: for 42 of 100 letters, the most similar letter in the Berlin collection comes from the same city, against 29 by chance and 30 for the ratio; without Byblos the figures are 29 against 16. The pairs are telling. EA 287 is closest to EA 286, both from Jerusalem; EA 157 is closest to EA 60, the two letters of Amurru made of Mount Lebanon clay; EA 281 is closest to EA 283, both from Gath. The counter-example confirms the rule: EA 285, sent by Abdi-Ḫeba but made of clay from the Beth-Shean area, is closest to a letter from Gath, not to the other Jerusalem letters. What the silhouette adds here is thickness: Byblos letters are tall and thin (thickness a quarter of the width, against a third for the other vassals).

Third, the form of a letter followed the place where the tablet was made more closely than the scribe who wrote it. The seven letters of Abdi-Ḫeba of Jerusalem are the work of one scribe, on the evidence of script, language and scribal marks [@moranAmarnaLetters1992; @vitaCanaaniteScribesAmarna2015; @mandellCanaaniteScribalCreativity2026, 487]. Two of them were made elsewhere: EA 285 of clay from the Beth-Shean area and EA 291 of the typical Gezer fabric [@gorenInscribedClayProvenance2004]. These two are close in proportion to the letters made in those places (1.17 against 1.21 for Beth-Shean; 1.27 against 1.33 for Gezer), not to the scribe's Jerusalem letters. The scribe of Rib-Hadda of Byblos offers the opposite case. EA 138, written at Beirut by a Byblian scribe [@gorenInscribedClayProvenance2004; @mandellCanaaniteScribalCreativity2026, 175], is the tallest letter in Berlin (2.84), taller even than the letters made at Byblos and unlike the two other letters made at Beirut, which are wide (EA 98 of Yapaḫ-Hadda, 0.90; EA 143 of Ammunira, 0.85). One reading is that the Jerusalem scribe wrote on tablets prepared where he was, while Rib-Hadda's scribe formed his own. Lauinger has already suggested, from rulings that cut across the text, that some letters were ruled before inscription, perhaps by someone other than the scribe [@lauingerContributionDiplomaticsAmarna2019; @mandellCanaaniteScribalCreativity2026, 403]. Tablet form gives a second, independent way to ask who prepared a tablet.

**A temple archive under changing kings: Eanna.** The Neo-Babylonian kings drew the temples ever closer to the crown. In Eanna, from about 584 BCE the temple was run by royal officials rather than priests, and the kings pressed Eanna to follow the procedures of Esangila, as Nabonidus did soon after his accession, ordering offerings "as in Esangila and Ezida" (YOS 6, 10) [@kleberTempelUnd2008; @jursaOustingNurSins2018; @jursaUrukeanPriestsNeoBabylonian2019]. Diplomatic studies of the archive have linked tablet format to function: notes and archival records are wide, lists and letters tall [@kleberTabletFormatBookkeeping2021; @levaviDiplomaticsNeoBabylonian2021]. Pirngruber showed that the inventories of the temple's flocks changed from landscape to portrait format around the twenty-third year of Nebuchadnezzar, together with their terminology, and linked the change to the crown's tighter control [@pirngruberDiplomaticsApproachEanna2021]; Quillien found a change of format and layout in the textile accounts of the Ebabbar of Sippar in the same years [@quillienDiachronicChangeTablet2021]. The DigEanna project now gives text form, date and format for each tablet [@nabuccoDigEanna]. We merged its 4,290 records with the CDLI photographs (3,107 tablets measured; SI Dataset S4). The project's labels and our measurements agree: tablets it calls landscape have a median ratio of 0.72, those it calls portrait 1.69.

The archive as a whole changed in two steps (Fig. 7 C, D). Lists of animals and of people were often tall already under Nebuchadnezzar, a third to two fifths of them between 600 and 580 BCE and two in three by the 560s, in line with Pirngruber's inventories. Lists of staples and goods stayed wide for the whole reign, about one in five tall, and turned tall only under Nabonidus, reaching four in five by 540 BCE. The change holds within one collection (Yale lists: 0.71 under Nebuchadnezzar, 1.45 under Nabonidus). Lists of silver and of cult materials stayed wide, and the promissory notes kept their format throughout (0.73 to 0.75). The administrative notes, the commonest form of the archive, record single issues and receipts of silver, staples and goods. They are identified as "Note" by their operative phrases, chiefly *ina pāni* "at the disposal of", *maḫir* "received" and *našû* "has taken". These notes kept a ratio of about 0.70 for four decades and became less wide from the 560s, reaching 0.74 under Nabonidus (819 notes before, 348 after), within each large collection (Goucher College 0.70 to 0.74, Yale 0.70 to 0.75, Newell 0.70 to 0.74, Nies 0.69 to 0.73). A typical pair from one collection is GCCI 2, 4 (Nabopolassar year 12, 26 × 38 mm) and GCCI 1, 280 (Nabonidus year 10, 33 × 44 mm). Here the silhouette adds nothing to the ratio: the Eanna tablets changed their proportion rather than their outline (SI Section S6.3). Our data thus extend the published result in two ways. The reform of about 584 BCE changed the documents of the flocks, as Pirngruber showed, but the bulk of the archive changed format only under Nabonidus, the period in which Jursa and Gordin placed the drive to bring the provincial temples into line with Esangila [@jursaUrukeanPriestsNeoBabylonian2019].

**What the silhouette measures.** The twelve dimensions of the VAE were learned without any instruction about tablets, yet they correspond to features a scholar would recognise: one follows proportion, one thickness, and one how rectangular the tablet is and how square its corners (SI Section S6.4). These are features that catalogues rarely record and that are slow to measure by hand on thousands of tablets. A set of twelve outline and thickness measurements, computed from the same images, matches the VAE in most comparisons, which shows that the VAE has found real physical traits rather than artefacts of photography. The silhouette helps where provinces or cities differ in how they formed a tablet (Ur III, Amarna) and not where a scribal office changed only the proportion of its documents (Eanna; Fig. 8).

# Discussion

**Does the state make the document?** The tablets give three answers, one for each scale at which a format was set. At the scale of the state, the answer is yes, but late and only where the state also trained and checked its scribes. The Ur III provinces kept their own formats for the same type of text for most of the dynasty and converged, in proportion and in the whole outline, only under Šū-Suen and Ibbi-Suen, when the bala taxation and the state calendar bound the provinces to the crown. Format followed integration; it did not found it. The same holds for the Neo-Babylonian temple: the bulk of the Eanna archive changed its format only under Nabonidus, the king who pressed Eanna to follow Esangila. The most uniform tablets of all, however, were made at Early Dynastic Šuruppak and Adab, before any territorial state existed, so a single institution could set a norm as strict as any state. Neo-Assyria is the counter-example. The strongest state of its time left the most varied documents, because its scribes gave each type of text its own format, tall letters and legal documents and wide administrative records, as Radner showed [@radnerRelationFormat1995]. Variety here is a rule rather than its absence, and the state made the document only in the sense that its offices fixed one form per purpose.

At the scale of the place, the Amarna letters show that the form of a tablet followed where it was made more closely than who wrote it. The Jerusalem scribe's letters made at Beth-Shean and Gezer take the proportions of those places, and the place of making groups the letters more closely than their ratio alone. Scribes writing to the Pharaoh also chose a taller tablet than at home, a form for an international addressee that no letter states. At the scale of the office, Old Babylonian tablets follow the type of document first and the archive second, and city and reign only weakly. The Eanna archive changed in two steps, the documents of the flocks around 584 BCE and those of the storehouse under Nabonidus, so even a reform imposed by the crown reached each office in its own time.

**What scribes did, not what they said.** The school texts and lexical lists teach signs, words and model contracts; they say nothing about how wide to make a tablet. Yet from the Sargonic to the Old Babylonian period, scribes held the width of a document and let its height grow with the text, while before and after these centuries a tablet kept its proportion at every size. This is a rule of practice that no text records. In the same way, the Old Babylonian document types kept their forms across cities, which suggests that the model contracts of the school carried a form as well as a formulary [@spadaSumerianModelContracts2018]. Under the Achaemenids, Babylonian scribes kept the Neo-Babylonian format while the scribes of Persepolis kept a form inherited from Elamite Anšan. Empire changed the language and the masters of the documents, not the hands that formed them.

**Deeplomatics among the other lines of evidence.** Philology names the scribe, petrography the clay, and the measured form of the tablet, what we call deeplomatics, adds the craft between them. Classical diplomatics, from Mabillon to the Assyriological studies of particular archives [@mabillonDeReDiplomatica1681; @radnerRelationFormat1995; @jursaDiplomaticsLateBabylonian2021], described forms tablet by tablet; measured on the whole corpus, its observations become claims that can be tested and dated. The Amarna letters show how the three lines meet: Goren's clay groups, the philological identification of scribes and the outline of each tablet together suggest who prepared a tablet, a question that Lauinger raised from the rulings of the letters [@lauingerContributionDiplomaticsAmarna2019] and that Badreshany and Philip's call for macroscale evidence of manufacture invites [@badreshanyCeramicStudiesPetrographic2020]. Distant viewing does not replace close reading. Every pattern reported here was checked against named tablets, and several apparent results did not survive that check.

**The museum as a layer of the archive.** The silhouettes also record the history of the tablets after antiquity. They reveal which museum holds a tablet, because excavation, the antiquities market and the division of finds sent the tablets of one city and period to a few collections, and because each collection photographed its tablets in its own way [@charpinPublicationDocumentsArchives2026; @deboerDesertDesk2026]. Museum archaeology treats a collection as an archaeological context of its own, formed by acquisition, documentation and photography [@stevensonIntroductionMuseumArchaeology2022; @klammPhotographicPracticesMuseum2022]. For cuneiform, this layer is visible at scale: the Neo-Assyrian tablets cannot yet be told apart from the British Museum's photography of them. We therefore tested every result within museum or across museums held out from training, and report where the two disagree. The other limits are familiar to every epigrapher. Breakage is uneven across periods and is controlled as a variable, photographs carry no scale, and period and genre labels are inherited from the catalogues.

## Conclusions

The silhouette dataset we have released (92,876 CDLI tablets, each described by twelve VAE dimensions) can be read as a set of diplomatic measurements. Measured against direct measurement of the photographed views of 47,714 well-preserved tablets, the twelve numbers recover three features reliably: the proportion of the obverse (R² = 0.82) and the thickness of the tablet against its width, at the sides (0.71) and at the top and bottom (0.70). Two more are recovered in part, enough to compare groups of tablets but not to describe a single one: how rectangular the obverse is (0.51) and how square its corners are (0.46). Features that depend on the exact line of the outline, such as roundness, taper or breaks, are not recovered (SI Section S6.4). Of these features, the CDLI catalogue records thickness for 15% of the same tablets and the shape of the corners for none. The case studies show that they carry history: square against rounded corners separate Puzriš-Dagan from Girsu receipts of identical proportion, and thickness separates the letters of Byblos from those of the other vassals of the Pharaoh. A notebook and a one-page guide released with the article return these measurements for every tablet in the dataset, with the reliability of each. Tablet form, measured this way, answers the question with which we began: the state made the document where it also made the scribe.

# Materials and Methods

**Corpus and labels.** The corpus is the set of CDLI tablets photographed by April 2024 [@cdliContributors2024], released with their silhouettes, period labels and VAE codes (Data availability). Non-tablet objects (cones, cylinders, bullae, seals) were removed, leaving 87,783 tablets. Periods, genres, findspots, museums and catalogue dimensions come from the CDLI catalogue, checked against the live database in October 2026 (SI Section S1). External labels were joined by museum or publication number: Old Babylonian archives, text types and dates from Archibab, queried in October 2026 [@charpinAssyriologistComputerArchibab2014]; Ur III text types from the Database of Neo-Sumerian Texts [@molinaDatabaseNeoSumerianTexts]; Neo-Babylonian labels from Prosobab and DigEanna [@waerzeggersProsobabProsopographyBabylonia2019; @nabuccoDigEanna]; Amarna clay groups from Goren et al. [@gorenInscribedClayProvenance2004] and senders from Moran [@moranAmarnaLetters1992].

**Silhouettes and damage.** Each photograph was thresholded and the tablet views kept as a binary mask, as in the released dataset, and height-to-width ratios were measured on the silhouette. A damage index was fitted by logistic regression on outline features (extent, solidity, holes and number of parts) to the CDLI preservation labels and applied to every tablet (SI Section S2).

**Catalogue dimensions.** For Section 4, catalogue heights and widths were checked against the photographs: entries whose ratio is the inverse of the photographed one were swapped, and implausible ratios dropped. Archival documents are administrative, legal and epistolary tablets that are not school texts (60,207 tablets). Uniformity is the median absolute deviation of the log ratio from the period median, with bootstrap intervals; it was recomputed within museum, within findspot and by damage tercile (SI Section S5).

**Shape codes.** The VAE was trained on 512 × 512 masks with a twelve-dimensional latent space and an auxiliary period loss [@kingmaVAE2013]. Because this loss uses period labels, every uniformity and neighbour result was repeated on DINOv2 features of the same masks, which use no labels [@oquabDINOv2LearningRobust2023]; the two agree (Spearman 0.83 for the uniformity ranking). Twelve outline and thickness measurements were computed directly from the six views of each tablet, and ridge regression with cross-validation shows which of them the VAE recovers (SI Section S6.4).

**Tests.** Period identification uses logistic regression on DINOv2 features of the silhouettes (twelve periods, at most 3,000 tablets each), with five-fold cross-validation, either random or with museums held out; gradient-boosted trees on the VAE codes give the same picture (SI Section S3). Label signals (Figs. 3 and 4) compare the mean distance between tablets that share a label and are held in different museums with the same distance after permuting labels within museum, reported as a ratio with a permutation p-value. Group assignment in the case studies (Fig. 8) uses regularised logistic regression on standardised features within one museum, with balanced groups, repeated five-fold cross-validation and a null from 200 label permutations. All analyses run from scripts released with the article.

**Data, materials and software availability.** Silhouettes, ratios and VAE codes: Zenodo, 10.5281/zenodo.11668220 and 10.5281/zenodo.12787746. Code, notebooks and derived tables: github.com/DigitalPasts/ShapingHistory.
