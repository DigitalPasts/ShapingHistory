**Significance.** For three thousand years the clay tablet was the main written record of ancient Western Asia, yet its form has been described one tablet at a time. Measuring the outline of nearly 88,000 photographed tablets, we show that the form of a document followed the institution that trained and checked its scribes more than the state that ruled them. The provinces of the Ur III state converged only late, Neo-Assyrian scribes kept varied formats under a strong empire, and letters sent to the Pharaoh followed the place where the clay was formed. The approach makes diplomatics, the study of how documents were made, a measurable historical science, and the released measures give proportion, thickness and corner shape for tablets that no catalogue describes.

**Abstract.** Did the states of ancient Western Asia shape how their scribes made documents? Historians have long said so, on the evidence of a few hundred tablets at a time. We test the claim on 87,783 photographed cuneiform tablets in the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative, from the late fourth millennium to the first century BCE, each reduced to a silhouette without signs or surface. Silhouettes identify a tablet's period in 57% of cases across twelve periods, but part of this signal is the photography of the museum that holds it, a modern layer of the archive that we control throughout. Measured on 60,207 documents, the tall tablet was a Babylonian norm from the Sargonic to the Old Babylonian period, while wide tablets appear in Elam around 1400 BCE and in Babylonia only in the first millennium. Uniformity does not simply follow the state. The most uniform tablets come from Early Dynastic Šuruppak and Adab, before any territorial state; the Ur III provinces converged only in the last two reigns; and Neo-Assyrian documents, made under the strongest state of their time, are the most varied. Three case studies place the norm at different levels: the state in Ur III, the place where the tablet was made in the Amarna letters, and the temple office at Eanna under Nabonidus. Tablet form records what scribes did rather than what they said about their craft.

**Keywords:** cuneiform | diplomatics | distant viewing | scribal practice | variational autoencoder

# Introduction

Did the states of ancient Western Asia shape how their scribes made documents? Historians of nearly every period of cuneiform writing have said yes, usually in passing. The Ur III state (2110 to 2003 BCE) is described as a bureaucracy whose uniform paperwork reflects central control [@steinkellerAdministrativeEconomicOrganization1987], while others read the same records as the work of households whose practices the state merely scaled up [@garfinkleWasUrIII2008]. Middle Assyrian tablets are read as instruments of a state bureaucracy [@postgateMiddleAssyrianTablets1986; @cancik-kirschbaumMiddleAssyrian2012], Hittite formats as conventions of the royal chancery [@waalHittiteDiplomaticsStudies2015], Neo-Assyrian letters as keeping one format across archives [@radnerRelationFormat1995], and late Babylonian documents as evidence of institutional norms under empire [@hacklMaterialienZurUrkundenlehre2013; @jursaDiplomaticsLateBabylonian2021]. Each claim rests on a few hundred tablets from one period. None has been tested across the history of the script, so none can tell whether uniformity follows the state or comes before it.

The difficulty lies partly in the sources. What we know about scribal training comes mostly from what scribes chose to write about it: school compositions, lexical lists and colophons [@wilckeWerUndSchrieb2000; @charpinReadingWriting2010; @veldhuisLevelsLiteracy2011]. The tablets themselves are evidence of another kind. Each was shaped from clay by a trained hand before any writing started, so its form is a trace of practice rather than a statement about it.

Reading such traces together is what distant viewing makes possible: the systematic, explicit and reproducible application of computational methods to large visual corpora, so that patterns invisible in any single object become measurable [@arnoldTiltonDistantViewing2019; @arnoldTiltonDistantViewingBook2023]. It requires a code system decided before the analysis. Ours is deliberately minimal: each tablet is reduced to its silhouette, without signs, text or surface, and we look at the corpus whole before the archive and the single tablet, the telescope before the microscope [@gordinMattersGenreStyle2025].

There are good reasons to expect that silhouettes carry history. Shaping the clay was part of scribal education, and tablet format changed with the stage of training, the genre and the use of a text [@veldhuisCuneiformTabletEducational1996; @delneroArchivesLibrariesOld2019; @taylorTabletsArtefactsScribes2011]. Reading institutions from the external form of documents is the founding idea of diplomatics, from Mabillon's *De re diplomatica* (1681) to the modern science of how records are made [@mabillonDeReDiplomatica1681; @durantiDiplomaticsNew1998]. Assyriologists have described tablet format by genre, archive and period [@radnerRelationFormat1995; @jursaDiplomaticsLateBabylonian2021], but in terms such as "cushion-shaped" that resist comparison, and on corpora that one expert can examine. Distant viewing turns these categories into explicit measures and applies them to every photographed tablet.

We apply it to 87,783 photographed tablets in the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative (CDLI), from the late fourth millennium to the first century BCE [@cdliContributors2024]. We ask whether a tablet's period can be read from its silhouette, how tablet proportions changed, and how uniform each period, site and archive was, measured by the ratio of height to width and in a twelve-dimensional shape space learned by a variational autoencoder [VAE; @kingmaVAE2013]. We test the answers in three case studies that hold the type of document constant while something else changes: the provinces of the Ur III state, the letters sent to the Pharaoh in the fourteenth century BCE, and the Eanna temple archive at Uruk under the Neo-Babylonian kings. Silhouettes discard the surface cues that inflate earlier classifiers [@chenDistributionShift2024], but not the habits of the museums that photographed the tablets, and we control for these throughout.

# Results

## The corpus through the telescope

The corpus is very uneven (Fig. 1). Four periods, Ur III, Neo-Assyrian, Old Babylonian and Middle Babylonian, hold 82% of the tablets, and administrative documents make up 48% of all tablets and more than 85% of every period before 2000 BCE. Excavation and the antiquities market concentrated whole periods in single sites [@rattenborgArchaeologicalDistributionCuneiform2023]: 99% of Old Assyrian tablets come from Kanesh [@larsenAncientKanesh2015], 96% of Neo-Assyrian tablets from Nineveh and 78% of Middle Babylonian ones from Nippur, so a period's profile may be that of one institution. We therefore compare like with like, administrative tablets within a period and sites within a period wherever we can. Most tablets are also damaged. Rather than set them aside, which would leave almost no Neo-Assyrian material, we treat preservation as a variable: a damage index computed from each outline separates complete tablets from fragments (AUC 0.92), and 68% of Neo-Assyrian tablets fall in the most damaged third against 4% of Ur III ones. Controlling for it leaves the ranking of periods by uniformity unchanged. Photographs give proportions, not sizes, but where CDLI records dimensions (17,568 tablets) the photographed and physical ratios agree (Spearman 0.77; SI Section S1).

## What silhouettes tell us about chronology

The proportion of height to width is a choice the scribe made before writing: it fixes how many lines fit on a face and how the tablet sits in the hand [@taylorTabletsArtefactsScribes2011]. A classifier trained on silhouettes names the period of an unseen tablet in 57% of cases across twelve periods, from Early Dynastic IIIb to the Achaemenid empire (SI Fig. S2). Part of this is the museum. CDLI's images were made over two decades by scanning, studio photography and museums' own photographs [@dahlLookingForwardBack2019], and the tablets of one period and city went to a few collections [@charpinPublicationDocumentsArchives2026], so among Ur III tablets from Umma the model names the right museum three times out of four (chance one in five). Tested only on museums it never saw, the classifier falls to 30%. Ur III tablets are still recognised (72% before, 69% after); Neo-Assyrian recognition collapses from 86% to almost nothing, because 99% of these tablets are in the British Museum. Ur III is the clearest case of a shape norm shared across collections. The ranking of periods by uniformity survives this control (SI Section S3).

The errors are informative. Achaemenid tablets from Persepolis are recognised 94% of the time, those from Babylonia only 33%, because most look Neo-Babylonian, in line with the continuity of Babylonian scribal practice under Persian rule [@jursaDiplomaticsLateBabylonian2021]. Within the Old Babylonian period, linked to the Archibab database [@charpinAssyriologistComputerArchibab2014], shape follows document type first: loans, receipts and leases now in different cities and museums are more alike than chance. Archive comes next (the tablets of Šamaš-hazir, now in six museums, resemble each other), while city and reign matter little (SI Section S4). Across periods, resemblance follows regions rather than a Babylonian genealogy: the administrative tablets of Middle Elamite Anšan find almost a quarter of their closest matches in the Persepolis Fortification archive, nine times the expected share [@stolperTextsTalliMalyan1984; @henkelmanAchaemenidHeartland2012], and Old Assyrian letters from Kanesh resemble Middle Assyrian letters from Assur.

## Tablet proportions across three millennia

Tablets are known to have grown wider over time [@taylorTabletsArtefactsScribes2011; @radnerRelationFormat1995]. Measured on 60,207 administrative, legal and epistolary tablets, the picture is more articulated (Fig. 2). Early Dynastic tablets were nearly square (1.00 to 1.02). The tall tablet (portrait / Hochformat) is a norm of the Sargonic to Old Babylonian centuries (Old Akkadian 1.21, Ur III 1.13, Old Babylonian 1.28). Middle Babylonian tablets return to the square (0.95), and only in the first millennium do Babylonian documents become wide (landscape / Querformat; Neo-Babylonian 0.73). Assyrian tablets stay square from Old Assyrian Kaneš to Middle Assyrian Aššur (1.02), and the Middle Elamite tablets of Haft Tepe are already wide around 1400 BCE (0.77), seven centuries before Babylonia [@herreroHaftTepeChoix1990].

**How strict each norm was.** Uniformity, the typical distance of a period's tablets from its median (Materials and Methods), ranks Early Dynastic IIIa first (0.038, mostly Šuruppak and Adab), followed by first-millennium Babylonia (Achaemenid 0.067, Neo-Babylonian 0.080) and Ur III (0.079), within museum, within findspot and across levels of damage. Neo-Assyrian documents are the most varied (0.36), because letters and legal documents are tall and administrative records wide, as Radner showed (1.34, 1.27 and 0.86) [@radnerRelationFormat1995]. Behind Ur III uniformity lie provinces with formats of their own. Puzriš-Dagan made the smallest and most uniform receipts (Nisaba 8, 299, 25 × 27 mm, British Museum), while a Girsu messenger text is tall and narrow (HLC 163, 45 × 30 mm, ISAC Chicago) and its Umma counterpart square (Nisaba 27, 228, 28 × 26 mm, Yale) [@manderMessengerTextsGirsu2008; @dagostinoUmmaMessengerTexts2008]. Compared within the same type of text [@molinaDatabaseNeoSumerianTexts], the difference between Girsu and Umma shrinks from Šulgi to Ibbi-Suen, when it disappears: format followed the integration of the provinces, as is known for their calendars and taxes [@widellCalendarNeoSumerianUr2004; @sharlachProvincialTaxationUr2004]. Old Babylonian is less uniform because it had two formats: records of a single transaction are near square, documents about land and persons tall (OECT 15, 189 at 1.07 against OECT 15, 268 at 1.58, both Larsa, Ashmolean Museum). The tall family is the one kept as evidence, often in envelopes and jars [@veenhofCuneiformArchivesIntroduction1986], so format told the archivist what a document was; in first-millennium Babylonia, one wide format served every type.

**Scribes fixing the proportions.** Ranking the documents of each period by size (SI Section S5.3) shows when scribes kept a fixed balance, the same proportion at every size [@georgeGeometryEarlyQuranic2010]. In Early Dynastic Girsu and in the first millennium they did. From the Sargonic to the Old Babylonian period, however, scribes held the width and let the height grow with the text: Old Babylonian documents vary in width only between about 31 and 55 mm but in height between 32 and 91 mm. A tablet held in one hand set the width, the text the height.

## Three archives: state, court and temple

**The state and its provinces: Ur III.** Among the messenger texts of the Harvard Museum of the Ancient Near East, the silhouette assigns a tablet to Girsu or Umma correctly in 93 cases out of 100, against 83 for the ratio alone and 50 by chance. A Girsu messenger text is tall, narrow and, for its width, thick (MVN 11, 109); its Umma counterpart nearly square and thinner (Nisaba 27, 12). Receipts from Puzriš-Dagan and Girsu at Harvard share one proportion (1.10 and 1.11), yet the silhouette separates them in 83 cases out of 100: Puzriš-Dagan receipts have square corners, Girsu receipts rounded ones (Fig. 4). The signal is not universal: at ISAC Chicago, catalogue height and width separate Umma from Puzriš-Dagan receipts better than the silhouette (SI Section S6.1). Within the same type of text, the distance between Girsu and Umma silhouettes is stable from Šulgi to Amar-Suen and falls by a third under Šū-Suen and by two thirds under Ibbi-Suen, when the crown's hold on the provinces is documented through bala taxation and the state calendar [@sharlachProvincialTaxationUr2004].

**Writing at the edge of the Babylonian world: the Amarna letters.** Moran's edition fixes the sender of most letters [@moranAmarnaLetters1992], and the petrographic study of Goren, Finkelstein and Na'aman located the clay of 274 of them [@gorenInscribedClayProvenance2004]. Badreshany and Philip note that some neighbouring fabrics cannot be told apart under the microscope and that provenance gains most when joined to macroscale features of manufacture [@badreshanyCeramicStudiesPetrographic2020]. Tablet form is such a feature. We measured 198 photographed letters, 176 of them in the Vorderasiatisches Museum, Berlin (Fig. 3A, B; SI Dataset S3).

First, scribes writing to the Pharaoh used taller tablets than at home: Babylonian letters sent to Egypt (1.53) against Middle Babylonian letters from Nippur (1.33), Mittani letters (1.54) against those of Nuzi (1.06), and EA 15 of Aššur-uballiṭ I (1.40, Metropolitan Museum of Art 24.2.11) [@metEA15] against Middle Assyrian letters from Aššur (1.10). Second, letters made in one place resemble each other: 131 Berlin letters fall into 17 places of making that differ in proportion, from Byblos (1.49, 36 letters) to Gaza (1.19), and for 42 of 100 letters the most similar letter in Berlin comes from the same place, against 29 by chance. What the silhouette adds is thickness: Byblos letters are tall and thin. Third, form followed the place of making more closely than the scribe. The one scribe of Abdi-Ḫeba of Jerusalem [@vitaCanaaniteScribesAmarna2015; @mandellCanaaniteScribalCreativity2026, 487] wrote EA 286 (1.87) and EA 287 (2.18) at home, but EA 285 and EA 291, made of clay from Beth-Shean and Gezer, take the proportions of those places (1.17 and 1.27). Rib-Hadda's scribe offers the opposite case: EA 138, written at Beirut by a Byblian scribe [@mandellCanaaniteScribalCreativity2026, 175], is the tallest letter in Berlin (2.84), unlike the two other Beirut letters (0.90, 0.85). The Jerusalem scribe may have written on tablets prepared where he was, Rib-Hadda's scribe on his own. Lauinger suggested from rulings that some letters were prepared by someone other than the scribe [@lauingerContributionDiplomaticsAmarna2019]; tablet form gives a second, independent test.

**A temple archive under changing kings: Eanna.** From about 584 BCE Eanna was run by royal officials, and Nabonidus pressed it to follow the procedures of Esangila [@kleberTempelUnd2008; @jursaUrukeanPriestsNeoBabylonian2019]. Pirngruber showed that the inventories of the temple's flocks turned from landscape to portrait around Nebuchadnezzar's twenty-third year [@pirngruberDiplomaticsApproachEanna2021]. Merging the DigEanna records [@nabuccoDigEanna] with the CDLI photographs (3,107 tablets) shows that the archive changed in two steps (Fig. 3C, D). Lists of animals and people were tall by the 560s; lists of staples and goods stayed wide under Nebuchadnezzar and turned tall only under Nabonidus, reaching four in five by 540 BCE, also within one collection (Yale lists 0.71 to 1.45). The administrative notes, identified by the operative phrases *ina pāni*, *maḫir* and *našû*, became less wide from 0.70 to 0.74 in every large collection. The reform of about 584 BCE changed the documents of the flocks; the bulk of the archive changed only under Nabonidus.

**What the silhouette measures.** The twelve VAE dimensions were learned without instruction about tablets, yet one follows proportion, one thickness, and one how rectangular the tablet is and how square its corners (SI Section S6.4). Twelve outline and thickness measurements computed from the same images match the VAE in most comparisons, so the VAE has found physical traits rather than photographic artefacts. It helps where places differ in how they formed a tablet (Ur III, Amarna), not where an office changed only the proportion of its documents (Eanna; Fig. 4).

# Discussion

**Does the state make the document?** The tablets answer at three scales. At the scale of the state, yes, but late and only where the state also trained and checked its scribes. The Ur III provinces converged only under the last two kings: format followed integration rather than founding it. The bulk of the Eanna archive changed only under Nabonidus, who pressed the temple to follow Esangila. Yet the most uniform tablets of all were made at Early Dynastic Šuruppak and Adab, before any territorial state, and Neo-Assyria, the strongest state of its time, left the most varied documents, because its offices gave each type of text its own format [@radnerRelationFormat1995]. At the scale of the place, the Amarna letters followed where the tablet was made more than who wrote it, and scribes chose a taller tablet for the Pharaoh than at home, a convention that no letter states. At the scale of the office, Old Babylonian tablets follow the type of document and the archive more than city or reign, and even a reform imposed by the crown reached each office of Eanna in its own time.

**What scribes did, not what they said.** School texts teach signs, words and model contracts, but not how wide to make a tablet. Yet from the Sargonic to the Old Babylonian period scribes fixed the width and let the height grow, and the document types kept their forms across cities, which suggests that model contracts carried a form as well as a formulary [@spadaSumerianModelContracts2018]. Under the Achaemenids, Babylonian scribes kept their format and the scribes of Persepolis an Elamite one: empire changed the masters of the documents, not the hands that formed them.

**Deeplomatics and its limits.** Philology names the scribe, petrography the clay, and the measured form of the tablet adds the craft between them. Diplomatics described forms tablet by tablet; measured on the whole corpus, its observations become claims that can be tested and dated, as the Amarna letters show when clay, scribe and outline are read together. The silhouettes also record the life of the tablets after antiquity. Museum archaeology treats a collection as an archaeological context formed by acquisition, documentation and photography [@stevensonIntroductionMuseumArchaeology2022; @klammPhotographicPracticesMuseum2022], and this layer is visible at scale: Neo-Assyrian tablets cannot yet be told apart from the British Museum's photography of them. We therefore tested each result within museum or on held-out museums. Breakage, photographs without scale and inherited catalogue labels are the other limits.

**Conclusions.** The released dataset describes 92,876 tablets by twelve VAE dimensions. Against direct measurement of 47,714 well-preserved tablets, they recover proportion (R² 0.82) and relative thickness (0.71 at the sides, 0.70 at the ends) reliably, and how rectangular a tablet is and how square its corners in part (0.51, 0.46). The CDLI catalogue records thickness for 15% of these tablets and corners for none. A notebook and a one-page guide return these measures for every tablet. Measured this way, tablet form answers our question: the state made the document where it also made the scribe.

# Materials and Methods

**Corpus and labels.** We used the CDLI tablets photographed by April 2024 [@cdliContributors2024], with non-tablet objects removed (87,783 tablets), and catalogue data checked against the live database in October 2026. External labels were joined by museum or publication number: Archibab, queried in October 2026 [@charpinAssyriologistComputerArchibab2014], the Database of Neo-Sumerian Texts [@molinaDatabaseNeoSumerianTexts], Prosobab and DigEanna [@waerzeggersProsobabProsopographyBabylonia2019; @nabuccoDigEanna], and the Amarna clay groups and senders [@gorenInscribedClayProvenance2004; @moranAmarnaLetters1992].

**Measures.** Silhouettes are the binary masks of the released dataset. A damage index was fitted by logistic regression on outline features to the CDLI preservation labels. Catalogue heights and widths entered the other way round were corrected against the photographs. Uniformity is the median absolute deviation of the log height-to-width ratio from the period median, with bootstrap intervals, recomputed within museum, findspot and damage tercile. The VAE has a twelve-dimensional latent space and an auxiliary period loss [@kingmaVAE2013]; because that loss uses labels, uniformity and neighbour results were repeated on label-free DINOv2 features [@oquabDINOv2LearningRobust2023], which agree (Spearman 0.83). Twelve outline and thickness measurements were computed from the six views, and cross-validated ridge regression shows which the VAE recovers.

**Tests.** Period identification uses logistic regression on DINOv2 features of the silhouettes (twelve periods, at most 3,000 tablets each), with five-fold cross-validation, either random or with museums held out; gradient-boosted trees on the VAE codes give the same picture (SI Section S3). Label signals compare distances between tablets that share a label but sit in different museums with distances after permuting labels within museum. Case-study assignments use regularised logistic regression within one museum, balanced groups, repeated cross-validation and permutation nulls. Details are in SI Sections S1 to S6.

**Data, materials and software availability.** Silhouettes, ratios and VAE codes: Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.11668220 and 10.5281/zenodo.12787746. Code, notebooks and derived tables: github.com/DigitalPasts/ShapingHistory.
