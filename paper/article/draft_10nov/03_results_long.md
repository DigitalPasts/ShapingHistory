
## What the polity scores do and do not predict

If the reach of a state made its documents uniform, the most powerful polities should have the most uniform tablets. Across the fourteen periods, none of the three indices predicts uniformity (Fig. 3; SI Appendix, S10). For the proportion of archival tablets, the institutional economy index (B) has the strongest relation, in the expected direction, but it is weak and uncertain (Spearman ρ = −0.43, permutation p = 0.12; ρ between −0.68 and −0.04 across 2,000 redraws of the scores), and it weakens within museum (ρ = −0.35, p = 0.21). For the whole outline, polity reach (A) and control of writing (C) relate in the opposite direction: within museum, the polities that reached furthest and regulated writing most left somewhat *more* varied tablets (ρ = 0.47 and 0.49, p = 0.09 and 0.07). A tablet-level regression with museum fixed effects and the wild cluster bootstrap over the fourteen periods gives the same picture (all p > 0.2).

The periods that break the expected pattern explain why. The most uniform archival tablets of all come from Early Dynastic IIIa (c. 2600-2500 BCE), mostly from Šuruppak and Adab, made under city-states with the lowest reach score of the series (A = 1.4). The Neo-Assyrian empire (911-612 BCE), the polity with the greatest reach (A = 3.0) and among the strongest control of writing (C = 2.9), left the most varied documents. Its scribes gave each type of document its own format, tall letters and legal documents and wide administrative records, as Radner showed [@radnerRelationFormat1995], and our measurements confirm it (median height-to-width ratios 1.34, 1.27 and 0.86). Ur III (2110-2003 BCE) is uniform and highly centralised, but its uniformity comes largely from one agency, Puzriš-Dagan, whose receipts are the smallest and most regular tablets of the period (a typical receipt measures 25 × 27 mm; Nisaba 8, 299, British Museum). The number of institutions that wrote the documents of a period, and the number of formats they kept, matters more than how far the state reached. This is the expectation of the institutional model of the Introduction, and it is why the turning points that changed form were those that changed the institutions.

## Who set the form: document, archive, museum

Turning points tell us when form changed; archives tell us who kept it. We took the archive of each tablet from the specialist databases (Archibab, NaBuCCo, Prosobab, the Oracc catalogues, the provinces of the Database of Neo-Sumerian Texts) and, within each of the 20 archives with at least 50 photographed tablets, let the shape features form groups of their own. We then asked which of the known facts about the tablets those groups follow: type of document, holding museum, findspot, reign, decade or degree of damage (Fig. 4; SI Appendix, S11).

The type of document comes first. It is the strongest or the only significant field in 11 of the 20 archives, from Ur III Girsu and Umma (c. 2050 BCE) to the Old Babylonian houses of Merkes at Babylon (normalised mutual information 0.37) and the Neo-Babylonian family archive of Ea-ilūtu-bāni at Borsippa (0.26), the Achaemenid business house of the Murašû at Nippur (0.18) and the Middle Assyrian archive M 4 at Assur (0.16) [@pedersenArchivesLibrariesCity1985]. The reign is the strongest field in none of them; it is significant only at Puzriš-Dagan and in the unprovenanced Ur III tablets, and there it is small (0.01 to 0.02). Within one archive, the scribes of successive kings went on making each kind of document as before. This is why the turning points that changed form were those that changed institutions rather than kings: the king could change without the archive noticing.

The holding museum is the second field, and it marks the archives that modern history has divided. The Puzriš-Dagan tablets, dispersed through the antiquities market from the early twentieth century to 44 museums in our sample, form shape groups that follow the museum more than anything else (0.22, against 0.03 for the type of document); Šamaš-ḫazir's archive, now in six museums, follows type (0.18), decade (0.16) and museum (0.13) together. Across archives, the same holds: the distances between the Ur III provincial archives follow their museum mix (Mantel ρ = 0.66, p = 0.009) and their dates (0.47, p = 0.04) more than their mix of document types, while the Middle Assyrian archives of Assur, excavated and kept together, differ by the types of document they hold (0.52, p = 0.003). The counter-examples are archives of one kind of document, such as the Ur III archive of queen Šulgi-simti and the Neo-Assyrian tablets of the Northwest Palace at Kalḫu, room ZT 4, whose shape groups follow nothing we can name.

The Old Babylonian period (2003-1595 BCE) shows the pattern most clearly. In the Archibab archives, tablets that share a document type resemble each other across cities and museums more than tablets that share a city or a reign: loans, receipts and leases from different collections are markedly more alike than chance, letters and purchases less so (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Specialists recognise a loan or a sale at a glance, and even small private archives standardised their formats [@goddeerisShapeSizeArchival2023]; what is new is that this can be measured across cities and collections, and that it outweighs city and reign. Archive comes next. The tablets of Šamaš-ḫazir, who managed the royal domain of Larsa for Hammurabi after 1763 BCE [@fietteArchibab3Palais2018], are now in six museums, yet they resemble each other more than other tablets of the same types in those museums. The counter-example is expenditure lists and summary accounts, which show no shared form. Text length explains at most a seventh of their variation. More likely, these modern categories group records that scribes kept apart, such as single disbursements and monthly or yearly summaries, which at Nippur differ in form [@goddeerisShapeSizeArchival2023]. Model contracts used in the Nippur school taught the document types [@spadaSumerianModelContracts2018], which may explain why each type kept its form across cities.

Holding museum is the third layer. Excavation, the antiquities market and the division of finds sent the tablets of one archive to many collections [@charpinPublicationDocumentsArchives2026; @deboerDesertDesk2026], and each collection photographed its tablets in its own way [@dahlLookingForwardBack2019]. Where an archive is split, museum explains part of the shape groups; where it is held in one place, it cannot. Museum archaeology treats a collection as an archaeological context of its own [@stevensonIntroductionMuseumArchaeology2022; @klammPhotographicPracticesMuseum2022], and for cuneiform this layer is visible at scale: a classifier names the museum of an Ur III tablet from Umma three times out of four (chance one in five). Every result in this article was therefore tested within museum, and those that did not survive were withdrawn.

## Three millennia of form and its geography

Seen whole, the corpus confirms that tablet form is legible at scale, and shows where. A classifier given only the silhouette names the period of a tablet it has not seen 57% of the time across twelve periods (chance 8%), and 30% when tested on museums it never saw. Mapped by findspot, recognition follows the centres of the scribal tradition (Fig. 5). In the third millennium BCE the Ur III provincial capitals, Girsu, Umma, Nippur and Puzriš-Dagan, are recognised most reliably, and their norm held across collections (72% before and 69% after museums were held out). In the second millennium, recognition is high at Kanesh and in the Old Babylonian cities and low at Alalakh and Ḫattuša, whose tablets do not resemble any one heartland tradition. In the first millennium, Nineveh is recognised almost perfectly, but this falls to almost nothing when the British Museum is held out: 99% of these tablets are in that museum, and the model had learned its photography. The Achaemenid tablets of Pārśa (Persepolis) are recognised 94% of the time, those from Babylonia only 33%, because most Babylonian tablets of the Persian period look Neo-Babylonian, in line with the continuity of Babylonian scribal practice under Persian rule [@waerzeggersMardukremanniLocal2014; @jursaDiplomaticsLateBabylonian2021].

The map also shows which traditions travelled. Joined to the heartland site whose archival tablets are nearest in median shape, the peripheral sites fall along regional lines rather than toward the political centre of their time. Kanesh, the Old Assyrian merchant colony in Anatolia, is nearest to the Middle Assyrian tablets of Kar-Tukulti-Ninurta, and Old Assyrian letters are matched with Middle Assyrian letters from Assur nine times as often as chance predicts (e.g. TCL 19, 65 and VS 1, 105). Middle Elamite Anšan (Tall-i Malyan) finds almost a quarter of its closest matches in the Persepolis Fortification archive, nine times the expected share (Malyan 59 is closest to PF 102) [@stolperTextsTalliMalyan1984]: Achaemenid administration in Fārs continued Elamite practice [@henkelmanAchaemenidHeartland2012], and its tablets carry that inheritance across six centuries. The counter-examples are informative too. The Alalakh tablets' nearest match is Ur III Ur, a resemblance that follows the British Museum rather than history, and Pārśa's nearest heartland site is Nippur, a weak link once Anšan is included.

Measured on 60,207 administrative, legal and epistolary tablets with catalogue dimensions, the proportions themselves changed in more than one step (Fig. 6).

![**Tablet proportions across three millennia.** The median administrative, legal or epistolary tablet of each period, drawn to scale from catalogue dimensions and placed at its date; rows group scribal traditions; numbers give the median height-to-width ratio (SI Appendix, S5).](FIGS/Fig5_optionA_tablet_strip.png){#fig-shift} The tall tablet is a norm of the Sargonic to Old Babylonian centuries (c. 2350-1600 BCE; median height-to-width ratios 1.21 Old Akkadian, 1.13 Ur III, 1.28 Old Babylonian). Before it, the Early Dynastic tablets of Šuruppak, Girsu and Ebla were nearly square (1.00 to 1.02); after it, Middle Babylonian tablets return to the square (0.95), and only in the first millennium do Babylonian documents become wide (Neo-Babylonian 0.73, Achaemenid 0.77). Assyrian tablets stay square from Kanesh (1.02) to Middle Assyrian Assur (1.02), and the Middle Elamite tablets of Haft Tepe are wide around 1400 BCE (0.77), seven centuries before Babylonia adopts this format [@herreroHaftTepeChoix1990]. Within these centuries scribes planned their tablets differently. From the Sargonic to the Old Babylonian period they held the width and let the height grow with the text: Old Babylonian documents vary in width only between about 31 and 55 mm but in height between 32 and 91 mm. Before and after, a tablet kept its proportion at every size, as in Early Dynastic Girsu (VS 14, 22 at 35 × 35 mm and VS 14, 57 at 85 × 85 mm, Vorderasiatisches Museum), a fixed balance of the kind manuscript scholars measure in codices [@georgeGeometryEarlyQuranic2010] (SI Appendix, S5). No text records this rule. It is a habit of the hand, and it was learnt in the same institutions whose reorganisation the turning points date.
