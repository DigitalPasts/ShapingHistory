Alternative titles: "When the state reached the scribe: political turning points in cuneiform tablet form, 2400 to 480 BCE"; "Institutions, not rulers: political turning points in the form of cuneiform tablets"; "Measuring administration at scale: political change and the form of cuneiform tablets". The title follows the turning-point story as ShG asked; "Reform, not conquest" holds for the two robust reforms and the two conquests, while successions and collapses are mixed, so a more cautious title may be preferred.*

**Significance.** States leave their mark on the documents they produce, but historians usually see that mark one archive at a time. We measured the form of 87,783 photographed cuneiform tablets and dated more than 30,000 of them by reign or year, using the specialist databases of the field. Tested against ten political turning points fixed in advance, tablet form changed when rulers reorganised or founded the institutions that wrote the documents, as under Urukagina of Lagaš and when Šulgi of Ur founded his livestock agency. It did not change detectably when a conqueror took over a working administration, as Hammurabi did at Larsa and Cyrus at Babylon. Tablet form records the institutions behind documents and dates the moments when political power reorganised them, at a scale no reading of texts allows.

**Abstract.** When did political change reach the people who wrote the documents of ancient states? We answer for cuneiform, the main writing system of Western Asia for three millennia, by measuring the form of 87,783 photographed tablets in the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative. Each tablet is reduced to a silhouette and described by learned shape features, checked against a label-free vision model, simple outline measures and catalogue dimensions. We dated 30,696 tablets to a reign or year by linking them to seven specialist databases, and scored the polities of fourteen periods on twelve parameters of centralization from 915 quoted statements in the historical literature. For ten political turning points fixed before the test, we compared tablets of the same site and document type written in the 20 years before and after each event, with damage and holding museum controlled. Tablet form shifted under every test when Urukagina reorganised the great household of Lagaš (c. 2351 BCE) and when Šulgi founded the agency of Puzriš-Dagan (2054 BCE). It did not shift detectably when Hammurabi took Larsa (1763 BCE), when Cyrus took Babylon (539 BCE) or when Esarhaddon succeeded his murdered father (681 BCE); for the other five events the evidence is mixed. A blind scan of the dated record finds its change points in the Ur III state and none in the Old Babylonian or first-millennium stretches. Within archives, form follows the type of document, never the reign, and across periods no index of state power predicts how uniform documents were. The form of a tablet records the institution behind it, and changes in form date the moments when political power reorganised that institution.

# Introduction

States govern through documents. Weber made written, rule-bound procedure the mark of bureaucratic domination [@weberEconomySociety1978]; Mann defined the infrastructural power of a state as its capacity to penetrate society and carry out decisions through its own institutions, as distinct from the despotic power of its ruler [@mannAutonomousPowerState1984; @mannSourcesSocialPower1986]; and Scott showed how states make their subjects legible by imposing standard formats on records, measures and names [@scottSeeingLikeState1998]. Each of these accounts predicts that political power leaves a trace in the form of documents, not only in their content. Historians of later periods have followed that trace. The growth of royal government in medieval England can be read in the multiplication and standardisation of its charters and rolls [@clanchyMemoryWrittenRecord1979], and the formats of letters and documents on papyrus changed with the administrations that used them [@sarriMaterialAspectsLetter2018]. Quantitative codicology has measured the proportions of thousands of manuscript pages to recover the rules of their makers [@bozzoloOrnatoHistoireLivreManuscrit1980; @georgeGeometryEarlyQuranic2010], and computational palaeography and diplomatics now classify scripts and documents at scale [@kestemontArtificialPaleography2017; @ambrosioDigitalDiplomatics2014]. None of this work, however, has linked the measured form of documents to dated political events across the history of a writing system.

Cuneiform offers the conditions for such a test. For three thousand years, from the late fourth millennium to the first century BCE, clay tablets were the main written record of Western Asia, and hundreds of thousands survive. Historians of nearly every period have tied their form to the state. Ur III paperwork, with its standard formats and year names, has been read as the product of a centralised bureaucracy [@steinkellerAdministrativeEconomicOrganization1987; @sallabergerUrIIIZeit1999] and also as the work of households whose practices the crown merely scaled up [@garfinkleWasUrIII2008]. Middle Assyrian and Hittite formats are described as conventions of the royal administration [@postgateMiddleAssyrianTablets1986; @waalHittiteDiplomaticsStudies2015], Neo-Assyrian letters as keeping one format across archives [@radnerRelationFormat1995], and Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid documents as evidence of institutional norms and their change under empire [@hacklMaterialienZurUrkundenlehre2013; @jursaDiplomaticsLateBabylonian2021]. The temple archive of Eanna at Uruk changed the format of its flock inventories when the crown tightened its control around 584 BCE [@pirngruberDiplomaticsApproachEanna2021]. Each claim rests on the inspection of a few hundred tablets from one period. None can say whether form changed when the state changed, because none follows the same measure across many states and many political events.

The tablets are evidence of a particular kind. What we know of scribal training comes mostly from what scribes and rulers chose to write about it: school compositions, lexical lists, colophons and royal hymns [@charpinReadingWriting2010; @veldhuisLevelsLiteracy2011]. These say what scribes claimed. A tablet was shaped from clay by a trained hand before any sign was written, so its form is a trace of practice rather than a statement about it [@taylorTabletsArtefactsScribes2011]. Reading institutions from the external form of documents is the founding idea of diplomatics, from Mabillon's *De re diplomatica* of 1681 to the modern science of records [@mabillonDeReDiplomatica1681; @durantiDiplomaticsNew1998], and Assyriologists have applied it to archives of every period [@veenhofCuneiformArchivesIntroduction1986; @radnerRelationFormat1995; @jursaDiplomaticsLateBabylonian2021]. Two limits have held it back: descriptive terms such as "cushion-shaped" resist systematic comparison, and the corpora rarely exceed what one expert can examine.

Distant viewing removes both limits. Arnold and Tilton define it as the systematic, explicit and reproducible application of computational methods to large visual corpora [@arnoldTiltonDistantViewing2019; @arnoldTiltonDistantViewingBook2023]. It obliges the researcher to fix a code system before the analysis, deciding what is measured and what is discarded, and it moves from the corpus seen whole to the archive and the single object, the telescope before the microscope [@gordinMattersGenreStyle2025]. Our code system is deliberately minimal. Each of 87,783 photographed tablets in the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative [@cdliContributors2024] is reduced to a black-and-white silhouette of its faces and edges, without signs or surface. Its form is described in three independent ways: by twelve features learned by a variational autoencoder (VAE) [@kingmaVAE2013], by a self-supervised vision model that never saw a period label (DINOv2) [@oquabDINOv2LearningRobust2023], and by simple outline measures (proportion, corners, thickness) that can be checked against the catalogue dimensions recorded in millimetres. What the learned features capture is therefore known, and every result can be stated in terms a diplomatist would use.

Two further steps make the political test possible. We dated 30,696 tablets to a reign or a year by joining the photographs to seven specialist databases, from the Database of Neo-Sumerian Texts to the Old Assyrian eponym list, and we scored the polity of each of fourteen periods on twelve parameters of centralization, each score resting on quoted statements in the historical literature (915 statements from 166 works). With dated tablets, form can be compared before and after a political event; with scored polities, form can be compared across states. Together they measure administration at a scale, and with a time resolution, that the reading of texts has not reached for cuneiform or, as far as we know, for any premodern writing culture.

## How political change reaches a document

A polity does not write its own documents. Tablets were written by scribes trained in households, temples, palaces and private families, and each of these institutions kept its own routines: how a receipt was laid out, how large an account was, how a letter was folded and sealed [@veenhofCuneiformArchivesIntroduction1986; @taylorTabletsArtefactsScribes2011]. Archaeologists read the standardisation of craft products as an index of who controlled production and how [@riceEvolutionSpecializedPottery1981; @blackmanStandardizationHypothesisCeramic1993; @eerkensTechniquesAssessing2001; @rouxCeramicStandardization2003], and a tablet is a craft product as much as a text. Political change reaches the form of a tablet only through the institution that made it.

This gives the framework for our scores and our test. We describe each polity on three dimensions, each with four parameters scored 0 to 3 from the literature (SI Appendix, Codebook). Dimension A, polity reach (territory, administrative hierarchy, fiscal extraction, communications), measures Mann's extensive power. Dimension B, institutional economy (the weight of palace, temple and private households, and royal control over the temples), asks who held the economy and its archives. Dimension C, control of writing and norms (bureaucratic procedure, schooling, law, standard measures and calendars), measures how far the state set the rules of record keeping itself, Weber's bureaucracy and Scott's legibility. The scheme draws on the Seshat global history databank where its variables fit [@turchinSeshatGlobalHistory2015], on state-capacity theory [@monsonFiscalRegimesPolitical2015], and on the debates of Assyriology about the temple economy and the reach of the early states [@gelbAllegedTempleState1969; @fosterNewLookSumerian1981; @steinkellerAdministrativeEconomicOrganization1987; @yoffeeMythsArchaicState2005]. If the state shaped documents through its reach, uniformity should follow A; if through its institutions and rules, it should follow B and C.

Political turning points then come in kinds that differ in what they do to the producing institution. A *reform* changes how existing institutions keep their records; a *foundation* creates a new central institution with its own scribes; a *conquest* puts a working administration under a new ruler; a *collapse* takes provinces out of the centre's hands; a *succession* replaces the king within the same state. Institutional theory leads us to expect form to change at the first two and at collapse, and to persist through conquest and succession. Administrative routines are path dependent: they persist because those who run them have invested in them, and they change at critical junctures when the rules of the institution itself are reset [@pierson2004politics; @capocciaKelemenCriticalJunctures2007]. A conqueror who keeps the local scribes inherits their routines. We fixed ten testable turning points from the standard histories before looking at the shape data, typed each one by what it did to the producing institution, and applied one test to all of them.
