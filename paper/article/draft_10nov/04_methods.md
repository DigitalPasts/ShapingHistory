
# Materials and Methods

**Corpus.** The corpus is the set of tablets photographed in the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative (CDLI) by April 2024, released with their silhouettes and shape codes (Data availability) [@cdliContributors2024]. Cones, cylinders, bullae and seals were removed, leaving 87,783 tablets. Period, genre, findspot (provenience), holding museum and catalogue dimensions come from the CDLI catalogue and were checked against the live database in October 2026; 0.85% of the tablets had been re-dated since 2024, which changes no result reported here (SI Appendix, S1). The main analyses use fourteen periods with enough archival tablets for comparison, from Early Dynastic IIIa (c. 2600 BCE) to the Hellenistic period; Hittite and Middle Elamite tablets, which come almost entirely from one site each (Ḫattuša and Haft Tepe), are reported in the SI. *Archival documents* are tablets that CDLI classes as administrative, legal, epistolary or business documents; literary, lexical and school tablets follow other rules [@veldhuisLevelsLiteracy2011] and are treated separately (SI Appendix, S5.4).

**Silhouettes.** Each photograph, usually a "fatcross" of obverse, reverse and four edges, was thresholded to a black-and-white mask of the tablet views, removing signs, surface, colour and background [@chenDistributionShift2024]. Three descriptions of the mask were used. (i) *Learned shape features*: a variational autoencoder (VAE) [@kingmaVAE2013] trained on 512 × 512 masks compresses each tablet to twelve numbers. Ridge regression against direct measurements shows what these numbers carry: the proportion of the obverse (R² = 0.82), the thickness at the sides (0.71) and at the ends (0.70), and in part how rectangular the obverse is (0.51) and how square its corners (0.46) (SI Appendix, S6.4). (ii) *A label-free check*: because the VAE was trained with an auxiliary period loss, every test was repeated on the embeddings of DINOv2 ViT-B/14 [@oquabDINOv2LearningRobust2023], a self-supervised model that never saw a period label, computed on the same masks at 224 px and reduced to 32 principal components (the first twelve explain 64% of the variance, and these were used where twelve dimensions were needed). (iii) *Simple measures*: the height-to-width ratio of the obverse, corner fill, rectangularity, taper and side and end thickness, measured directly on the views. For 17,568 tablets CDLI records height and width in millimetres; the photographic and catalogue ratios agree (Spearman 0.77), and catalogue entries whose ratio was the inverse of the photographed one were swapped. Photographs carry no scale, so sizes in millimetres come only from the catalogue.

**Damage.** Most tablets are chipped or broken, and breakage is uneven across periods (4% of Ur III tablets fall in the most damaged third of the corpus, against 68% of Neo-Assyrian ones). Rather than exclude broken tablets, a damage index was fitted by logistic regression on outline features (extent, solidity, holes and number of parts) to the CDLI preservation labels (AUC 0.92) and applied to every tablet. In all tests below, each shape feature is first residualised on damage (linear and quadratic terms), so that a change in how broken the tablets are cannot pass for a change in their form (SI Appendix, S2).

**Holding museum.** The silhouettes also identify the museum that holds a tablet: among Ur III tablets from Umma, for example, a classifier names the museum three times out of four (chance one in five). Two histories lie behind this, the dispersal of archives through excavation and the antiquities market, and the different photographic standards of the collections [@dahlLookingForwardBack2019; @charpinPublicationDocumentsArchives2026]. Every test was therefore run twice, once within strata of site and document type and once within strata of site, document type and holding museum, and the two are reported side by side (SI Appendix, S3).

**Dates.** CDLI records a king or year for part of the corpus. We added dates from seven specialist databases, joined to CDLI by museum and publication number: the Database of Neo-Sumerian Texts (BDTNS) for Ur III [@molinaDatabaseNeoSumerianTexts], Archibab for Old Babylonian [@charpinAssyriologistComputerArchibab2014], the Oracc project catalogues for Neo-Assyrian, Middle Assyrian and Hellenistic texts, the electronic Babylonian Library (eBL), NaBuCCo and Prosobab for Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid texts [@waerzeggersProsobabProsopographyBabylonia2019; @nabuccoDigEanna], and the Old Assyrian eponym list of the REL project, matched to the dated tablets of the Old Assyrian Research Environment (OARE). Where a database and CDLI disagree, the database date is used and the tablet is flagged (340 cases). Regnal years were converted to years BCE in the middle chronology [@sallabergerAssociatedRegionalChronologies2015]. In all, 30,696 archival and other tablets have a date in years BCE, 26,486 of them to the year and 4,210 only to a reign. A tablet dated only to a reign carries no year, so in every before-and-after comparison it was admitted only when the whole reign falls on one side of the comparison (for example, a tablet of Esarhaddon, 681-669 BCE, counts as "after" for an event in 681). The per-tablet dates, with their source and a digital citation of each database record, are released as SI Dataset S1.

**Polity scores.** The polities of the fourteen periods were scored on twelve parameters in three dimensions (A, polity reach: territory, administrative hierarchy, fiscal extraction, communications; B, institutional economy: palace, temple, royal control of temples, private households; C, control of writing: bureaucratic procedure, schooling, law, standardisation of measures and calendars). Each parameter was scored 0 to 3 in half points from quoted statements in the historical literature (915 statements from 166 works, each with its page reference), following a codebook that defines every level and states what evidence counts (SI Appendix, Codebook). Each score carries a distribution reflecting disagreement in the literature; S.G. reviewed all scores and statements in three rounds. Indices are the means of the parameters of each dimension, with private households reversed.

**Turning-point protocol.** Ten political events were fixed from standard histories before the shape data were examined, keeping those with at least 30 dated archival tablets on each side within 20 years at the same sites (SI Appendix, S8, lists the candidates that failed this rule). Each event was typed by what it did to the institutions that wrote the tablets: reform (Urukagina of Lagaš, c. 2351 BCE; Šulgi year 21, 2072 BCE), foundation (Šulgi year 39, the founding of Puzriš-Dagan, 2054 BCE), collapse (Ibbi-Suen year 3, when the provinces stopped dating by the king of Ur, 2024 BCE; Samsu-iluna's loss of the south, 1739 BCE), conquest (Hammurabi's conquest of Larsa, 1763 BCE; Cyrus at Babylon, 539 BCE) and succession or reorganisation within a state (Sargon II to Sennacherib, 705 BCE; Esarhaddon, 681 BCE; Darius I, 521 BCE). For each event, archival tablets of the 20 years before were compared with those of the event year and the 19 years after (by reign for Urukagina and for Sargon II against Sennacherib), only within strata of the same site and document type present on both sides with at least three tablets, so that a change in the mix of sites or genres cannot pass for a change in form. The effect on each feature is the weighted mean over strata of the difference between the medians after and before, in units of the robust standard deviation (1.4826 × median absolute deviation), with weights n_before·n_after/(n_before + n_after). The overall change in form is the length of the vector of the twelve shifts. Because this length is positive even when nothing changes, it is reported as an *excess* over its median under the null, and tested against 2,000 permutations of the before and after labels within strata (1,000 for DINOv2). Uniformity is compared as the weighted mean log ratio of the dispersions after and before. P-values are adjusted across events by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. *Power*: because the medians move exactly with a constant added to every tablet, a true change of size m in direction u moves the observed shift vector by m·u; power at size m is the share of permutation shift vectors s for which the length of s + m·u exceeds the 95th percentile of the null. We report power at the size of the change observed at the foundation of Puzriš-Dagan, and a pooled test of the conquests and successions (sum of their standardised excesses against the sum of independent permutation draws).

**Blind change-point scan.** To guard against choosing the events that work, the same comparison was run with 10-year windows at every year of four densely dated stretches (Ur III, 2068-2012; Old Babylonian, 1815-1722; Neo-Assyrian, 714-669; Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid, 606-524 BCE), with 300 permutations per year. A year is a change point if its p-value survives Benjamini-Hochberg correction within its stretch (q < 0.05) and its excess is the largest within five years. Change points were then compared with a list of 23 political events (the ten above and the accessions and reorganisations of the standard histories), counting a match within three years, against the share of scanned years that lie within three years of a listed event by chance. The scan was repeated within every site and document type with at least 200 dated tablets.

**Archives.** Archive labels come from Archibab, NaBuCCo, Prosobab, the Oracc catalogues (Pedersén's Middle Assyrian archives; Neo-Assyrian findspot archives), the BDTNS provinces for Ur III, and the CDLI archive field [@pedersenArchivesLibrariesCity1985; @pedersenArchivesLibraries1998]. Across archives, the archive of a tablet was predicted from its shape by leave-one-out nearest median against 200 label permutations, and the distances between archive medians were compared (Mantel test, Spearman, 2,000 permutations) with the distances in document-type mix, museum mix, site mix and median date. Within each archive of at least 50 tablets, k-means clusters of shape (k = 2-4 by silhouette width) were compared with document type, holding museum, reign, decade, damage and site by normalised mutual information against 1,000 permutations, Benjamini-Hochberg corrected.

**Uniformity and the polity scores.** The uniformity of a period is the median absolute deviation of the log height-to-width ratio of its archival tablets, pooled and within museum, and the median distance of their VAE vectors from the period median. Its relation to each index was measured by Spearman correlation over the fourteen periods, with p-values from 50,000 permutations of the index values; score uncertainty by redrawing the whole score table 2,000 times from the score distributions; and a tablet-level regression with museum fixed effects by the wild cluster restricted bootstrap with the fourteen periods as clusters (Webb weights, 9,999 draws), which keeps the right size with few clusters [@cameronGelbachMillerBootstrap2008; @webbReworkingWildBootstrap2023].

**Period recognition and the map.** A logistic regression on DINOv2 features of the silhouettes names the period of a held-out tablet (twelve periods, at most 3,000 tablets each, five-fold cross-validation, random or with museums held out). The map (Fig. 5) places each findspot at its Pleiades coordinates [@pleiades] and colours it by the share of its tablets recognised; dashed lines join a peripheral site to the heartland site whose archival tablets of the same millennium are closest in median shape.
