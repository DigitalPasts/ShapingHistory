
# Results

## Tablet form at ten political turning points

We applied one test to every event (Fig. 1; Materials and Methods). Archival tablets written in the 20 years before an event were compared with those written in the event year and the 19 years after, only within the same site and the same type of document, after removing the effect of damage. The change in form is measured on all twelve shape features at once, in units of their typical spread, and compared with the change that chance alone produces when the labels "before" and "after" are shuffled within each site and type. The test was run on the VAE features and repeated on DINOv2 features that never saw a period label, and each was repeated within museum. The events follow in date order.

**Lagaš under Urukagina (c. 2351 BCE).** The last Early Dynastic rulers of Lagaš left the largest archive of the third millennium before Ur III, about 1,800 tablets of the great household of the queen at Girsu [@schrakampReevaluatingReformsUrukagina2025; @selzUntersuchungenZurGotterwelt1995]. When Urukagina (Irikagina) took power from Lugalanda, his inscriptions proclaimed a set of measures long read as social reforms; recent re-evaluations read them as a reorganisation of the institutions of the city-state [@schrakampUrukaginaUndGeschichte2015; @schrakampReevaluatingReformsUrukagina2025]. Its clearest trace in the archive is institutional: the household of the queen (é-mí) became the household of the goddess Bau (é-dBa-ú), and its scribes went on recording the same work under the new name [@selzUntersuchungenZurGotterwelt1995]. The tablets of this household changed form. Comparing administrative tablets of Lugalanda and Enentarzi with those of Urukagina at the same sites, the change exceeds chance by 0.71 robust SD units with the VAE and 0.47 with DINOv2 (both p < 0.001, q ≤ 0.003), and it holds within museum (0.45 and 0.32, p < 0.001). The proportion of the tablets did not change (median height-to-width 1.02 before and after; shift −0.12 SD, p = 0.08): what changed is the outline, in seven of the twelve VAE dimensions (q < 0.05), among them the one that follows thickness, while the one that follows proportion did not move. The typical tablet before the reform, RA 11, 62 (Enentarzi year 6, State Hermitage Museum), and the typical tablet of Urukagina's household, HSS 3, 14 (Urukagina year 6, Harvard Museum of the Ancient Near East), have the same proportion and differ in how they were rounded. The tablets also became more varied under Urukagina (log dispersion ratio 0.12 with the VAE, 0.16 within museum, q ≤ 0.04), as one would expect while an institution's scribes adjust to a new order.

**Ur III: reform, foundation and the loss of the provinces (2072-2024 BCE).** Šulgi's middle years saw the measures that historians take as the making of the Ur III state: a standing army, a standard calendar and accounting procedures introduced across the provinces in the second half of his reign, from about his 21st year, and in his 39th year the foundation of Puzriš-Dagan, the crown's agency for livestock, with its own scribes and its own archive [@steinkellerAdministrativeEconomicOrganization1987; @sallabergerUrIIIZeit1999; @tsouparopoulouReconstructionPuzrisDagan2013; @sharlachProvincialTaxationUr2004]. The foundation of Puzriš-Dagan is the largest change in our record. Across seven sites and 14,000 tablets, form shifted by 0.71 SD beyond chance with the VAE and 0.48 with DINOv2 (both p < 0.001), and by the same amount within museum. The tablets became thinner at the sides and the ends (−0.20 and −0.26 SD, q = 0.01) and their corners fuller (+0.19 SD, q = 0.02), while their proportion stayed the same. This is the small, square-cornered receipt that the agency made by the tens of thousands (a typical example is Nisaba 8, 299, British Museum, 25 × 27 mm), and it appears at the other sites too, so the foundation of one agency changed how tablets were made across the provinces. Šulgi 21 is harder to test. Only 19 dated archival tablets survive from the 20 years before it at sites that continue after it, and the two representations disagree: DINOv2 finds a change (q = 0.035), the VAE does not (excess 0.06, p = 0.42). With such numbers the test could only detect a change three times larger than that at Puzriš-Dagan, and we leave the question open.

The Ur III state broke up from the third year of Ibbi-Suen, when the provinces stopped dating by the king of Ur: the archive of Puzriš-Dagan ends in his second year, those of Umma and Girsu within the next few years, and Nippur's by his eighth [@sallabergerUrIIIZeit1999; @garfinkleKingdomUr2022]. With DINOv2, tablet form changed at this juncture (0.26 SD beyond chance, p < 0.001, within museum 0.23), less than at the foundation of Puzriš-Dagan; with the VAE there is no change beyond chance (excess 0.06, p = 0.12; none within museum). We therefore treat the collapse as uncertain. The breakup undid in a few years a convergence that had taken decades. Girsu and Umma had kept their own formats for the same type of text, a tall messenger text at Girsu (HLC 163, 45 × 30 mm, ISAC Chicago) and a square one at Umma (Nisaba 27, 228, 28 × 26 mm, Yale Babylonian Collection) [@manderMessengerTextsGirsu2008; @dagostinoUmmaMessengerTexts2008], and the difference between them shrank steadily from early Šulgi to Ibbi-Suen, when it disappeared (SI Appendix, S5.5). Format followed the integration of the provinces, as their calendars and taxes did [@sharlachProvincialTaxationUr2004; @molinaArchivesBookkeepingSouthern2016]; it came late, and it did not outlast the state.

**Hammurabi and Samsu-iluna (1763 and 1739 BCE).** When Hammurabi of Babylon took Larsa in 1763 BCE, he annexed the largest kingdom of the south, and the written sources mark the change at once: Larsa's scribes dated by Hammurabi's year names, and his letters to his officials there survive in hundreds [@vandemieroopKingHammurabiBabylon2005; @charpinMesopotamienAltbabylonische2004]. But Hammurabi kept the royal domain of Larsa running under its own system, with officials such as Šamaš-ḫazir managing its fields and their scribes [@fietteDomaineRoyalHammurabi2018]. The tablets show no change: at the same five sites and types, the shift is no larger than chance with either representation (VAE excess 0.06, p = 0.33; DINOv2 −0.07, p = 0.76), and none appears within museum. Twenty-four years later, Samsu-iluna lost the south to rebellion and its cities were largely abandoned [@charpinMesopotamienAltbabylonische2004]. Here the evidence is mixed: no change at the same sites and types (p = 0.27 with the VAE, 0.78 with DINOv2), but a change within museum (0.35 and 0.26 SD beyond chance, p = 0.02 for both, q = 0.04 and 0.05). The earlier analysis of Hammurabi's reign found its strongest break at his accession, not at his conquest (SI Appendix, S8).

**Assyrian successions (705 and 681 BCE).** The Neo-Assyrian letters and documents of Nineveh allow two tests of succession within the strongest state of its time: the reign of Sargon II against that of Sennacherib, and the 20 years around the accession of Esarhaddon after the murder of his father [@radnerStateCorrespondenceAncient2014]. Esarhaddon's accession shows no change with either representation (VAE p = 0.33, DINOv2 p = 0.64). The reigns of Sargon II and Sennacherib differ with the VAE (excess 0.58, p < 0.001, q = 0.002, the same within museum) but not with DINOv2 (p = 0.34). This comparison is the weakest of the ten in design: it rests on one site and two types of document, Nineveh letters (1,031 of Sargon II against 63 of Sennacherib) and legal documents (14 against 128), and the letters of Sargon II are the state correspondence of governors writing from across the empire [@radnerStateCorrespondenceAncient2014], while those dated to Sennacherib are few and of other origin. Sennacherib also moved the court from Dur-Šarrukin to Nineveh, so the comparison spans a change of capital as well as of king. We report the result as a disagreement between the two representations and return to it in the Discussion.

**Cyrus and Darius in Babylonia (539 and 521 BCE).** Cyrus took Babylon in 539 BCE without a battle in the city, and Babylonian temples and families went on keeping their archives as before [@jursaNeoBabylonianEmpire2023; @waerzeggersMardukremanniLocal2014]. Darius I, after suppressing the Babylonian revolts of 522-521 BCE, reorganised the satrapies, yet the archives of Babylonia continued until the revolts against Xerxes in 484 BCE [@waerzeggersBabylonianRevoltsXerxes2003; @briantCyrusAlexander2002]. At neither event did tablet form change at the same sites and types (Cyrus: VAE p = 0.06, q = 0.14, DINOv2 p = 0.35; Darius: VAE p = 0.40, DINOv2 p = 0.64). Within museum, Darius I shows a change with the VAE (p = 0.02, q = 0.04), but on only 16 tablets after the event. The same continuity shows in recognition: most Babylonian tablets of the Persian period are classified as Neo-Babylonian.

**Could the tests have seen a change?** A test that finds nothing after a conquest means something only if it could have found something. The conquests and successions are smaller samples than the Ur III events, so we measured the power of each test (Materials and Methods; SI Appendix, S8). One by one, the tests of Hammurabi and Cyrus would have detected a change as large as the one at Puzriš-Dagan about two times in three with the VAE (67% and 59%) and less often with DINOv2 (41% and 37%). Taken together, the two conquests show no change (pooled p = 0.10 with the VAE, 0.57 with DINOv2), and the pooled test would have detected a Puzriš-Dagan-sized change at each of them 89% of the time with the VAE and 57% with DINOv2. Changes of that size at the conquests are therefore unlikely; smaller changes, of the size seen when the Ur III state broke up, cannot be excluded (power below 20%). The reforms and the foundation give a pooled signal that chance does not produce with either representation (p < 0.001). The successions and the collapses are where the two representations part: with the VAE the successions together show a change (p = 0.007, driven by Sargon II to Sennacherib) and the collapses do not (p = 0.13); with DINOv2 the reverse (p = 0.58 and 0.004). Of the ten events, then, two changed form under every test, the foundation of Puzriš-Dagan and Urukagina's reorganisation of the household of Lagaš; two conquests and one succession changed it under none; and five remain uncertain.

## Where the record changes when it is not told where to look

The ten events were fixed in advance, but a reader may still ask whether they were well chosen. We therefore scanned every year of the four densely dated stretches without reference to any event, comparing the ten years before each year with the ten years from it in the same way (Fig. 2).

In Ur III, the scan finds four change points, and three of them fall at junctures the historians name: 2053 BCE, a year after the foundation of Puzriš-Dagan; 2036 BCE, at the accession of Šū-Suen; and 2021 BCE, three years after the provinces began to break away. The fourth and strongest, 2064 BCE (Šulgi's 29th year), matches no listed event, and it rests on few tablets before it (122), at the point where the provincial archives of Umma and Girsu begin in earnest. Within single sites and types, the Ur III change points cluster in the same years: at Puzriš-Dagan in 2056 and 2033 BCE, at Umma in 2049 and 2027, at Ur in 2041, 2031 and 2021 BCE. The largest single change in the scan is at Puzriš-Dagan in the first years of Šū-Suen (2033 BCE, 1.65 SD beyond chance), when the agency's documents were reorganised. In the Old Babylonian, Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian to Achaemenid stretches the scan finds no change points at all. A first version of the scan had found breaks at 1792, 713 and 676 BCE; these came from tablets dated only to a reign being counted at the accession year of their king, and they disappeared once such tablets were admitted only where their whole reign fits (SI Appendix, S7).

Across all groups, 63% of the Ur III change points lie within three years of a listed event, against 49% expected by chance, a difference that 19 change points cannot establish (binomial p = 0.16). The scan thus confirms where the protocol found change, in the Ur III state between its founding agencies and its breakup, and where it did not, in the Old Babylonian and first-millennium stretches that contain the conquests of Hammurabi and Cyrus. It does not show that every listed event marks a break: the accessions of Amar-Suen and Šū-Suen appear at single sites, not everywhere.
