
# Discussion

**Where political change reached the document.** The tablets answer the question of the Introduction with a distinction that the written sources blur. Tablet form changed, under every test we applied, at two moments when rulers reset the institutions that kept records: when Urukagina reorganised the great household of Lagaš and placed it under the goddess Bau, and when Šulgi founded Puzriš-Dagan. It did not change detectably when a conqueror took over a working administration, as Hammurabi did at Larsa and Cyrus at Babylon, or when Esarhaddon succeeded his murdered father. For the conquests, the pooled test makes the claim precise: with the VAE features, a change as large as the one that followed the foundation of Puzriš-Dagan would have been detected nine times in ten, and none appeared; with the label-free features the test is weaker (57%). Smaller changes, of the size seen when the Ur III state broke up, cannot be excluded. The written record of these events is full of the language of rupture, of new kings, new year names, new oaths and new masters. The documents themselves were made by the same scribes in the same households, and their form says that, for the people who wrote them, conquest was often continuity.

The uncertain cases point the same way when they are read institutionally, although we fixed their types before the test and cannot claim them as confirmation. The change from Sargon II to Sennacherib, found with one representation only, spans the move of the court from Dur-Šarrukin to Nineveh, a refoundation of the central chancery rather than a mere change of king. The breakup of the Ur III state changed form with one representation and not the other, and the blind scan places its strongest late Ur III changes at Puzriš-Dagan in the first years of Šū-Suen, when the agency itself was reorganised. Whether a succession or a collapse changed the documents seems to have depended on whether it changed the offices that produced them.

This is the pattern that historical institutionalism predicts. Administrative routines are path dependent because those who run them have invested in them, and they change at critical junctures when the rules of the institution are themselves reset [@pierson2004politics; @capocciaKelemenCriticalJunctures2007]. Assyriologists have long argued for the continuity of Babylonian scribal practice under the Persians [@jursaDiplomaticsLateBabylonian2021; @waerzeggersMardukremanniLocal2014] and for Hammurabi's reliance on the existing administration of Larsa [@fietteDomaineRoyalHammurabi2018]; what the tablets add is that the continuity reached the hand that shaped the clay, and that it can be measured with the same test that detects a reform. The comparison with craft production is close. Archaeologists read the standardisation of pottery as a sign of who controlled its production, and of whether the makers were attached to an institution or worked independently [@riceEvolutionSpecializedPottery1981; @costinCraftSpecialization1991; @blackmanStandardizationHypothesisCeramic1993]. Scribes were attached specialists par excellence. Their products changed when their institution changed, and only then.

**Why the strongest states did not make the most uniform documents.** The same distinction explains the cross-period result. If uniformity measured state power, Neo-Assyria would be the most uniform period and Early Dynastic IIIa among the least; the opposite is true. The polity scores capture what the literature says about the reach, the economy and the control of writing of each state, and none of them predicts how uniform its tablets were. What does predict it is the number of institutions and formats behind a period's documents. A single agency, such as Puzriš-Dagan in Ur III or the temple households of Early Dynastic Šuruppak, makes a period look uniform; an empire that gave every type of document its own format, as Neo-Assyria did [@radnerRelationFormat1995], makes it look varied. Weber's bureaucracy and Scott's legibility are visible here, but in the formulary and the format of each type of document, not in a single imperial standard [@weberEconomySociety1978; @scottSeeingLikeState1998]. The control of writing that the scores record was exercised through institutions, and it is the institutions, not the state, that the tablets record.

**The unit of diplomatic form.** Within archives the type of document comes before everything else, and the reign comes nowhere. This confirms, at the scale of thousands of tablets and twenty archives, what diplomatists of cuneiform have shown archive by archive: the scribe chose a form for a kind of document, and that choice was taught, repeated and kept [@veenhofCuneiformArchivesIntroduction1986; @goddeerisShapeSizeArchival2023; @jursaDiplomaticsLateBabylonian2021]. It also explains why the turning points that changed form were the institutional ones. A new king inherited the archive with its types; a reform changed the types, or the institution that kept them. The Eanna archive shows the mechanism inside one institution: the reform that put the temple under royal officials around 584 BCE changed the documents of the flocks first, and the bulk of the archive changed format only under Nabonidus, the king who pressed Eanna to follow the procedures of Esangila [@pirngruberDiplomaticsApproachEanna2021; @jursaUrukeanPriestsNeoBabylonian2019]. Even a reform imposed by the crown reached each office in its own time.

**What scribes did, not what they said.** The school texts and the royal hymns tell us what scribes and kings claimed about writing [@charpinReadingWriting2010; @veldhuisLevelsLiteracy2011]. The form of the tablets records what scribes did, including rules that no text states: from the Sargonic to the Old Babylonian period they held the width of a document and let its height grow with the text, while before and after they kept its proportion at every size. Tablet form is therefore evidence of a different kind from the texts it carries, closer to the archaeology of practice than to the history of ideas, and it can be set against the texts. Where the two agree, as at the foundation of Puzriš-Dagan, the tablets confirm that a reform reached the scribes; where they disagree, as at Hammurabi's conquest, they show the limits of what a conqueror changed.

**Distant viewing and its limits.** Measuring administration at this scale required three decisions that shape what can be seen. The silhouette discards the text, so it measures the craft of the scribe and not the content; the dates come from the databases that specialists have built over decades, and they are concentrated in Ur III (21,458 of 30,696 dated tablets), so the earlier periods are tested more finely than the later ones; and the photographs carry the history of the collections that made them, so every result must be tested within museum. Five of the ten events (Šulgi 21, Ibbi-Suen 3, Samsu-iluna, Sargon II to Sennacherib and Darius I) give different answers with the two representations of shape or with and without the museum control, and Šulgi 21 rests on 19 dated tablets before the event. We report them as uncertain rather than choose the representation that suits the argument. Several results that looked strong in earlier stages of this study did not survive the museum control or the correction of reign-only dates, and they are withdrawn (SI Appendix, S12). Computational palaeography and diplomatics have so far classified scripts and documents [@kestemontArtificialPaleography2017; @ambrosioDigitalDiplomatics2014]; the step taken here is to date change in the form of documents and set it against the political history of the states that produced them. The same protocol could be applied wherever dated documents are photographed in large numbers, from Greek papyri to medieval charters [@sarriMaterialAspectsLetter2018; @clanchyMemoryWrittenRecord1979; @bozzoloOrnatoHistoireLivreManuscrit1980].

## Conclusions

Over two thousand years of cuneiform administration, the form of a tablet changed when the institution that made it was reorganised or founded, and it persisted through conquests that left that institution in place; successions and collapses changed it only in some tests, as far as they touched the offices that wrote. Within archives, form followed the type of document rather than the reign, and across periods the uniformity of documents followed the number of producing institutions rather than the power of the state. Tablet form is thus a record of institutions, made by the people who staffed them, and its changes date the moments when political power reached them. The silhouettes, shape features, dates and polity scores released with this article make it possible to test any further turning point in the same way.
