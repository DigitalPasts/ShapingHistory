### Three archives: state, court and temple

[Draft v4, 27 Oct 2026 (round 5). Sources marked for ShG: (L) from the literature, (D) from our data, (D+L) our data confirming or extending a published result. Silhouette = the VAE description of the six photographed views of a tablet.]

**The state and its provinces: Ur III.** The provincial bureaucracies of Girsu, Umma and Puzriš-Dagan kept their own conventions, and their messenger texts have been studied province by province (L) [@sharlachProvincialTaxationUr2004; @manderMessengerTextsGirsu2008; @dagostinoUmmaMessengerTexts2008]. Section 4 showed that Girsu and Umma also kept their own proportions for the same type of text. The silhouette shows this more sharply (D). Among the messenger texts of the Harvard Museum of the Ancient Near East, labelled as such in the Database of Neo-Sumerian Texts [@molinaDatabaseNeoSumerianTexts], the silhouette assigns a tablet to Girsu or Umma correctly in 93 cases out of 100, against 83 for the ratio alone and 50 by chance (137 tablets from each province). A Girsu messenger text is tall, narrow and, for its width, thick (MVN 11, 109, 44 × 32 mm, late Šulgi); its Umma counterpart is nearly square and thinner (Nisaba 27, 12, 31 × 29 mm, Šū-Suen). Receipts show what the ratio misses. At Harvard, receipts from Puzriš-Dagan and Girsu have the same proportion (1.10 and 1.11), yet the silhouette separates them in 83 cases out of 100: the Puzriš-Dagan receipts have square corners, the Girsu receipts rounded ones. The signal is not universal. In the New York Public Library, receipts from Umma and Puzriš-Dagan are separated only weakly, and at ISAC Chicago the height and width in millimetres recorded in the CDLI catalogue separate them better than the silhouette does (SI Section S6.1; the photographs carry no scale, so size in millimetres can only come from the catalogue). Within the same text type, the distance between the Girsu and Umma silhouettes stays the same from Šulgi to Amar-Suen and falls by a third under Šū-Suen and by two thirds under Ibbi-Suen. The convergence of proportions described in Section 4 was thus a convergence of the whole tablet, and it came in the last two reigns, when the crown's hold on the provinces is documented through the bala taxation and the state calendar (L) [@sharlachProvincialTaxationUr2004; @widellCalendarNeoSumerianUr2004].

**Writing at the edge of the Babylonian world: the Amarna letters.** Two results on the Amarna letters have been established (L). The Amarna letters edition of Moran fixes the sender of most letters [@moranAmarnaLetters1992]. The petrographic study of Goren, Finkelstein and Na'aman located the clay of 274 letters: the great kings wrote at home, most Canaanite rulers used local clay, some letters were written at the Egyptian centres of Gaza and Beth-Shean, and the kings of Amurru moved from the slopes of Mount Lebanon to Ardata, Irqata and Sumur [@gorenPetrographicInvestigationAmarna2002; @gorenInscribedClayProvenance2004]. Badreshany and Philip, reviewing petrography in Levantine archaeology, treat this study as a reference for the clays of the Late Bronze Age Levant, but also mark the limits of the method: some neighbouring fabrics cannot be told apart under the microscope (their example is the pottery of Arqa and of the Byblos region), and provenance work gains most when it is joined to the visible, macroscale features of how an object was made [@badreshanyCeramicStudiesPetrographic2020]. The form of a tablet is such a feature, and the observations below add it to the clay evidence. Philologists, for their part, group the letters by scribe, with one to four scribes writing for a city in a generation [@vitaCanaaniteScribesAmarna2015; @cohenCuneiformWriting2018]. We add three observations (D) on the 198 photographed letters, 176 of them in the Vorderasiatisches Museum, Berlin, and map them together with the clay analysis (@fig-amarna; coordinates from Pleiades [@pleiades]; all letters, with their clay group and its reliability, in SI Dataset S3).

First, a scribe writing to the Pharaoh used a taller tablet than for letters at home. Babylonian letters sent to Egypt (1.53, six letters) are taller than the Middle Babylonian letters found at Nippur (1.33, 239 letters), and Mittani letters (1.54, four letters) are much taller than the letters of Nuzi, a city of the Mittani realm (1.06, 16 letters). Both pairs point the same way, although the numbers are small. Aššur may follow the same pattern. EA 15, the letter of Aššur-uballiṭ I, is not in CDLI, but the Metropolitan Museum of Art publishes its measurements (7.7 × 5.5 cm, a ratio of 1.40) and an open-access photograph, on which we measure 1.37 (MMA 24.2.11) [@metEA15]. Middle Assyrian letters found at Aššur have a ratio of 1.10 (12 letters). The other Assyrian letter, EA 16, in the Egyptian Museum (Cairo) is not photographed and could not be measured.

Second, the letters made in one place resemble each other. Placed where Goren and his colleagues located their clay, 131 Berlin letters fall into 17 places with three or more letters, and these differ in proportion (SI Section S6.2): the letters made at Byblos are tall (1.49, 36 letters), and the two well-preserved letters made at Jerusalem taller still (EA 286, 1.87; EA 287, 2.18), while the letters made in the Bašan (1.14), the Beqaʿ (1.21) and at Gaza (1.19) are barely taller than wide. The silhouette groups the letters more closely than the ratio: for 42 of 100 letters, the most similar letter in the Berlin collection comes from the same city, against 29 by chance and 30 for the ratio; without Byblos the figures are 29 against 16. The pairs are telling. EA 287 is closest to EA 286, both from Jerusalem; EA 157 is closest to EA 60, the two letters of Amurru made of Mount Lebanon clay; EA 281 is closest to EA 283, both from Gath. The counter-example confirms the rule: EA 285, sent by Abdi-Ḫeba but made of clay from the Beth-Shean area, is closest to a letter from Gath, not to the other Jerusalem letters. What the silhouette adds here is thickness: Byblos letters are tall and thin (thickness a quarter of the width, against a third for the other vassals).

Third, the form of a letter followed the place where the tablet was made more closely than the scribe who wrote it (D+L). The seven letters of Abdi-Ḫeba of Jerusalem are the work of one scribe, on the evidence of script, language and scribal marks [@moranAmarnaLetters1992; @vitaCanaaniteScribesAmarna2015; @mandellCanaaniteScribalCreativity2026, 487]. Two of them were made elsewhere: EA 285 of clay from the Beth-Shean area and EA 291 of the typical Gezer fabric [@gorenInscribedClayProvenance2004]. These two are close in proportion to the letters made in those places (1.17 against 1.21 for Beth-Shean; 1.27 against 1.33 for Gezer), not to the scribe's Jerusalem letters. The scribe of Rib-Hadda of Byblos offers the opposite case. EA 138, written at Beirut by a Byblian scribe [@gorenInscribedClayProvenance2004; @mandellCanaaniteScribalCreativity2026, 175], is the tallest letter in Berlin (2.84), taller even than the letters made at Byblos and unlike the two other letters made at Beirut, which are wide (EA 98 of Yapaḫ-Hadda, 0.90; EA 143 of Ammunira, 0.85). One reading is that the Jerusalem scribe wrote on tablets prepared where he was, while Rib-Hadda's scribe formed his own. Lauinger has already suggested, from rulings that cut across the text, that some letters were ruled before inscription, perhaps by someone other than the scribe [@lauingerContributionDiplomaticsAmarna2019; @mandellCanaaniteScribalCreativity2026, 403]. Tablet form gives a second, independent way to ask who prepared a tablet.

**A temple archive under changing kings: Eanna.** The Neo-Babylonian kings drew the temples ever closer to the crown (L). In Eanna, from about 584 BCE the temple was run by royal officials rather than priests, and the kings pressed Eanna to follow the procedures of Esangila, as Nabonidus did soon after his accession, ordering offerings "as in Esangila and Ezida" (YOS 6, 10) [@kleberTempelUnd2008; @jursaOustingNurSins2018; @jursaUrukeanPriestsNeoBabylonian2019]. Diplomatic studies of the archive have linked tablet format to function: notes and archival records are wide, lists and letters tall [@kleberTabletFormatBookkeeping2021; @levaviDiplomaticsNeoBabylonian2021]. Pirngruber showed that the inventories of the temple's flocks changed from landscape to portrait format around the twenty-third year of Nebuchadnezzar, together with their terminology, and linked the change to the crown's tighter control [@pirngruberDiplomaticsApproachEanna2021]; Quillien found a change of format and layout in the textile accounts of the Ebabbar of Sippar in the same years [@quillienDiachronicChangeTablet2021]. The DigEanna project now gives text form, date and format for each tablet [@nabuccoDigEanna]. We merged its 4,290 records with the CDLI photographs (3,107 tablets measured; SI Dataset S4). The project's labels and our measurements agree: tablets it calls landscape have a median ratio of 0.72, those it calls portrait 1.69 (D+L).

The archive as a whole changed in two steps (D; @fig-amarna C, D). Lists of animals and of people were often tall already under Nebuchadnezzar, a third to two fifths of them between 600 and 580 BCE and two in three by the 560s, in line with Pirngruber's inventories. Lists of staples and goods stayed wide for the whole reign, about one in five tall, and turned tall only under Nabonidus, reaching four in five by 540 BCE. The change holds within one collection (Yale lists: 0.71 under Nebuchadnezzar, 1.45 under Nabonidus). Lists of silver and of cult materials stayed wide, and the promissory notes kept their format throughout (0.73 to 0.75). The administrative notes, the commonest form of the archive, record single issues and receipts of silver, staples and goods. They are identified as "Note" by their operative phrases, chiefly *ina pāni* "at the disposal of", *maḫir* "received" and *našû* "has taken". These notes kept a ratio of about 0.70 for four decades and became less wide from the 560s, reaching 0.74 under Nabonidus (819 notes before, 348 after), within each large collection (Goucher College 0.70 to 0.74, Yale 0.70 to 0.75, Newell 0.70 to 0.74, Nies 0.69 to 0.73). A typical pair from one collection is GCCI 2, 4 (Nabopolassar year 12, 26 × 38 mm) and GCCI 1, 280 (Nabonidus year 10, 33 × 44 mm). Here the silhouette adds nothing to the ratio: the Eanna tablets changed their proportion rather than their outline (SI Section S6.3). Our data thus extend the published result in two ways. The reform of about 584 BCE changed the documents of the flocks, as Pirngruber showed, but the bulk of the archive changed format only under Nabonidus, the period in which Jursa and Gordin placed the drive to bring the provincial temples into line with Esangila [@jursaUrukeanPriestsNeoBabylonian2019].

**What the silhouette measures.** The twelve dimensions of the VAE were learned without any instruction about tablets, yet they correspond to features a scholar would recognise: one follows proportion, one thickness, and one how rectangular the tablet is and how square its corners (SI Section S6.4). These are features that catalogues rarely record and that are slow to measure by hand on thousands of tablets. A set of twelve outline and thickness measurements, computed from the same images, matches the VAE in most comparisons, which shows that the VAE has found real physical traits rather than artefacts of photography. The silhouette helps where provinces or cities differ in how they formed a tablet (Ur III, Amarna) and not where a scribal office changed only the proportion of its documents (Eanna).
