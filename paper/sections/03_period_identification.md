**What silhouettes tell us about chronology.** Assyriologists have long dated tablets by their look before reading them, and the physical form of a tablet is known to correlate with its date and function [@taylorTabletsArtefactsScribes2011; @radnerRelationFormat1995]. The proportion of height to width matters for cuneiform diplomatics because it is a choice the scribe made before writing: it fixes how many lines fit on a face, whether the text runs across the long or the short side, and how the tablet sits in the hand. Its distribution over three millennia is the subject of Section 4. What has been missing, as Taylor notes, is a comprehensive account of tablet form across the whole record. A classifier trained on the silhouettes offers one. Asked to name the period of a tablet it has not seen, it is right 57% of the time across twelve historical periods, from Early Dynastic IIIb (c. 2500 BCE) to the Achaemenid empire (331 BCE; @fig-confusion).

**Museums before periods.** The silhouettes can also reveal which museum holds a tablet: among Ur III tablets from Umma, for example, the model names the right museum three times out of four (chance is one in five). Two histories lie behind this insight: CDLI's images were made over two decades using different standards, including scanning, studio photography and museums' own photographs [@dahlLookingForwardBack2019; @chenClassificationPaleographic2024], and excavation, the antiquities market and dispersed archives sent the tablets of one period and city to a few museums [@charpinPublicationDocumentsArchives2026; @deboerDesertDesk2026]. Tested only on museums it never saw, the classifier falls from 57% to 30%. Ur III tablets are still recognised (72% before, 69% after), as are, in part, Old Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian and Old Babylonian ones. Neo-Assyrian recognition collapses from 86% to almost nothing: 99% of these tablets are in the British Museum, and the model had learned that museum's images, labelling 30% of its other tablets Neo-Assyrian. Ur III is the clearest case of a shape norm shared across collections; for Neo-Assyrian we cannot yet tell shape from photography. The ranking of periods by uniformity survives the museum control (SI Section S3).

**Calibrating the chronological signal.** The Achaemenid tablets from Persepolis are recognised 94% of the time, those from Babylonia only 33%, since most of the latter look Neo-Babylonian, in line with the continuity of Babylonian scribal practice under Persian rule [@waerzeggers_marduk-remanni_2014; @jursaDiplomaticsLateBabylonian2021]. In Early Old Babylonian, only royal inscriptions and cones are recognised, but not everyday documents, so the label marks a dynastic division [@charpinMesopotamienAltbabylonische2004] rather than a change in how ordinary tablets were made.

**Inside one period: shape follows the document.** Old Babylonian comprises several scribal traditions, with schools that differed from city to city [@robsonTabletHouseScribal2001; @tanretAsperaAdAstra2002; @wagensonnerLarsaSchoolsPalaeographic2019; @delneroArchivesLibrariesOld2019]. We linked 2,479 photographed Old Babylonian tablets to the Archibab database of Old Babylonian archives [@charpinAssyriologistComputerArchibab2014], queried in October 2026, and compared tablets that share a label but sit in different museums (@fig-ob-labels). Shape follows document type most closely: loans, receipts and leases from different cities and museums are markedly more similar than chance, letters and purchases less so. Specialists recognise a loan or a sale at a glance, and even small private archives standardised their formats [@goddeerisShapeSizeArchival2023]. What is new is that this can be measured across cities and collections, and that it outweighs city and reign. Archive comes next. The tablets of Šamaš-hazir, who managed the royal domain of Larsa for Hammurabi after 1763 BCE [@fietteArchibab3Palais2018], are now in six museums, yet resemble each other more than other tablets of the same types in those museums. Across museums, only Larsa and the Hammurabi years stand out among cities and reigns; within one museum, tablets of the same type from the same city are more alike, so a local habit sits on top of the shared form. The counter-example is expenditure lists and summary accounts, which show no shared form. Text length explains at most a seventh of their variation in shape. More likely, these modern scholarly categories group records that scribes kept apart, such as single disbursements and monthly or yearly summaries, which at Nippur differ in form [@goddeerisShapeSizeArchival2023] (SI Section S4). A millennium later the pattern recurs: Neo-Babylonian promissory notes now in different museums also resemble each other more than chance would predict [@waerzeggersProsobabProsopographyBabylonia2019].

**Regional lines rather than sequential genealogies.** Within Babylonia, later letters and legal documents are as close to the Old Babylonian forms as any other tablets of their period, once museum is controlled. The one exception is small: Kassite letters from Nippur in the Penn Museum (e.g. BE 17/1, PBS 1/2) are slightly closer to the Old Babylonian letter shapes than other Kassite tablets from Nippur. Where tablets do resemble those of another period, the resemblance follows regions (@fig-periphery). The clearest case confirms what historians expect. The administrative tablets of Middle Elamite Anšan (Tall-i Malyan) find almost a quarter of their closest matches in the Persepolis Fortification archive, nine times the expected share; Malyan 59, for example, is closest to PF 102 [@stolperTextsTalliMalyan1984]. Achaemenid administration in Fārs continued Elamite practice [@henkelmanAchaemenidHeartland2012], and its tablets carry that inheritance in their outline across six centuries. A northern line appears as well. Old Assyrian letters from Kanesh are matched with Middle Assyrian letters from Assur nine times as often as chance predicts, though such matches remain few (7 in 100; e.g. TCL 19, 65 is closest to the Assur letter VS 1, 105). The Middle Babylonian tablets of Nuzi resemble Middle Assyrian ones three times as often as expected [@postgateMiddleAssyrianTablets1986], and those of Ḫattuša are closer to their Babylonian and Assyrian contemporaries twice as often. Old Babylonian letters from Susa, such as MDP 18, 246, find most of their closest matches among later tablets, Kassite ones from Nippur and Neo-Babylonian ones from Borsippa and Uruk. The Amarna letters show no particular affinity, and the Alalakh tablets' resemblance to Ur III follows the British Museum rather than history. These groups are small, and regional traditions such as Emar's two tablet formats [@flemingTabletTerminologyEmar2009] have too few photographs to test. Genealogy, then, runs along regional lines rather than from period to period within Babylonia.
