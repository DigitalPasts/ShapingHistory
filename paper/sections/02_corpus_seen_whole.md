**The corpus through the telescope.** Seen whole, the photographed record of cuneiform consists of 87,783 tablets in the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative, of which 87,221 fall in seventeen periods with at least 100 tablets each, from Uruk III (c. 3200 BCE) to the Hellenistic period [@cdliContributors2024; @fig-corpus]. The numbers are very uneven. Four periods, Ur III, Neo-Assyrian, Old Babylonian and Middle Babylonian, account for 82% of the tablets, while six periods have fewer than 500 each. Genres are just as uneven. CDLI assigns them along the conventional Assyriological division between archival documents (administrative, legal, letters) and the literary and scholarly texts of the "stream of tradition" [@oppenheimAncientMesopotamia1977; @pedersenArchivesLibraries1998]. Administrative documents make up 48% of all tablets and more than 85% of every period before 2000 BCE, while a quarter of the tablets, among them 65% of the Neo-Assyrian ones, have no genre assigned at all. An average over the whole corpus is therefore mostly an average of administrative tablets from four periods, and we compare like with like: uniformity is measured within administrative tablets wherever their numbers allow.

The shape of the corpus is also the shape of what survived and of how it was found. Cuneiform tablets are unevenly spread in time and space, because of ancient conditions of preservation and destruction as much as chance discoveries, excavation priorities and the antiquities market, which filled museums with tablets from a few sites [@rattenborgArchaeologicalDistributionCuneiform2023; @michelIdentifyingMissingEvidence2025]. Old Assyrian is the clearest case. 99% of its tablets come from Kanesh in Anatolia, where the merchants' archives were preserved in their houses in the lower town, while the contemporary archives of Assur itself remain largely unexcavated [@larsenAncientKanesh2015]. In the same way, 96% of Neo-Assyrian tablets come from Nineveh, 83% of Hittite tablets from Ḫattuša and 77% of Middle Assyrian tablets from Assur. Another 15% of all tablets have no recorded findspot. For many of these, the findspot can be reconstructed from publications, museum records or internal evidence such as month names and prosopography [@brumfieldKeyCity2019], but this is an extra step between the catalogue and any historical inference of the kind attempted here. Where one site dominates, a period's profile may be the profile of one institution. Middle Babylonian tablets, for example, come 78% from Nippur, so what we measure as Middle Babylonian practice is largely the practice of Nippur's administration, which might not represent the norm of the Kassite state. We therefore compare periods where several sites contribute, and sites within a period wherever we can.

The tablets also reach us in very different states. Most cuneiform tablets are chipped, cracked or abraded, and many are fragments. Rather than set broken tablets aside, which would leave almost no Neo-Assyrian material, we treat preservation as a variable, much as epigraphers read a damaged text against its parallels. A damage index computed from each silhouette's outline separates tablets catalogued as complete from fragments (AUC 0.92). Damage is unevenly spread: 4% of Ur III tablets fall in the most damaged third of the corpus, against 68% of Neo-Assyrian and 85% of Hittite tablets (@fig-corpus). It lowers the accuracy of period identification from 0.85 for the least damaged tablets to 0.74 for the most damaged, but controlling for it leaves the ranking of periods by uniformity unchanged.

Our code system reduces each photograph to a black-and-white silhouette of all its views, usually obverse, reverse and four edges. Signs, surface, colour and background are discarded. What remains is the outline the scribe shaped, without the photographic cues that inflate earlier classifiers [@chenDistributionShift2024]. Photographs give proportions, not sizes. CDLI does record sizes in millimetres for 17,568 of our tablets, however, and for these the photographic and physical height-to-width ratios agree closely (Spearman 0.77), though less so for broken tablets. All catalogue data were checked against the live CDLI database in October 2026. Since the period labels used here were fixed in April 2024, CDLI has re-dated 0.85% of the tablets, which leaves every period's measured uniformity practically unchanged. The extraction steps, their known errors and the full period table are in Materials and Methods and SI Table S1.
